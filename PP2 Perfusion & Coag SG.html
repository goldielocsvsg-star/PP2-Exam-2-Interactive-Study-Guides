What Is Coagulation?

Coagulation is the process by which blood changes from a fluid state into a solid clot to plug injuries to the vascular system. It occurs through hemostasis — the physiologic process to stop bleeding. Clotting factors are produced in the liver, most requiring Vitamin K.

ℹ
Clinical Relevance: Liver disease → impaired clotting factor synthesis → bleeding disorders. Warfarin blocks Vitamin K → blocks Factors II, VII, IX, X → therapeutic anticoagulation. This is the mechanistic link between the disease and the drug.
1
Stage 1 — Platelet Plug Formation

When a vessel is injured, subendothelial collagen is exposed. Platelets adhere, activate, and aggregate to form a temporary plug that must later be reinforced by fibrin.

Activators of Platelet Aggregation

  • Thrombin — most potent activator
  • Collagen — from damaged vessel wall
  • Thromboxane A2 (TXA2) — potent vasoconstrictor; blocked by aspirin
  • PAF — platelet activating factor
  • ADP — adenosine diphosphate; blocked by clopidogrel

Drug–Cascade Connections:
• Warfarin — blocks Factors II, VII, IX, X (Vit K–dependent) → monitored with INR
• Heparin (UFH) — enhances antithrombin → inactivates Thrombin + Xa → monitored with aPTT
• DOACs (apixaban, rivaroxaban) — directly block Factor Xa → no routine monitoring
• Dabigatran — directly blocks Thrombin (IIa) → no routine monitoring
▲
Virchow's Triad — Three Conditions Promoting Thrombus Formation

Any combination of these conditions significantly increases pathologic thrombus risk. The more factors present, the higher the DVT/PE probability.

🩹
Endothelial Injury
Damage exposes collagen → activates platelets and cascade

Examples: Surgery, trauma, IV catheters, hypertension, atherosclerosis
🐌
Circulatory Stasis
Slow flow allows clotting factors to concentrate

Examples: Immobility, bed rest, atrial fibrillation, heart failure
🩸
Hypercoagulability
Increased tendency to clot

Examples: Cancer, pregnancy, OCP, genetic disorders, COVID-19, dehydration
⚠
Clinical Pearl: Post-operative patients who are immobile (stasis) + had vascular injury (endothelial damage) + possibly dehydrated (hypercoagulability) have all three Virchow factors simultaneously → mandatory anticoagulant prophylaxis.

Risk Factors

Modifiable

  • Immobility / prolonged bed rest
  • Recent surgery (within 3 months)
  • Central venous catheters
  • Obesity, oral contraceptives
  • Active infection / sepsis / COVID-19

Non-Modifiable

  • Advanced age
  • History of prior DVT/PE
  • Genetic hypercoagulable state
  • Pregnancy
🩺
Signs & Symptoms
⚠
Classic Presentation: Sudden dyspnea + pleuritic chest pain + tachycardia + ↓ SpO2 = PE until proven otherwise
  • Sudden onset dyspnea — most common
  • Sharp, stabbing, pleuritic chest pain
  • Tachycardia and tachypnea
  • Apprehension — feeling of impending doom
  • Hemoptysis (blood-tinged sputum)
  • Diaphoresis, light-headedness, cough
  • Crackles, pleural friction rub
  • S3 or S4 heart sounds (RV strain)
  • Low-grade fever; decreased SpO2

Acute Treatment
  • Oxygen — maintain SpO2 >90%
  • Heparin (UFH or LMWH) — immediate anticoagulation; prevents clot extension
  • Alteplase — for massive PE with hemodynamic instability only
  • Semi-Fowler's position (facilitates breathing)
  • Continuous SpO2, cardiac rhythm, BP monitoring
🛡
Prevention
  • SCDs (sequential compression devices) — all surgical patients
  • Compression stockings
  • LMWH (enoxaparin) — post-op prophylaxis
  • Heparin (UFH) — low-dose SQ prophylaxis
  • DOACs or warfarin — long-term therapy post-PE
  • IVC filter — when anticoagulation contraindicated
📊
Improvement vs. Worsening
✔ Signs of Therapeutic Response
  • SpO2 improving toward ≥95%
  • Respiratory rate slowing toward normal
  • Chest pain intensity decreasing
  • Anxiety and restlessness resolving
  • Heart rate returning toward 60–100
  • D-dimer trending downward
  • Improved mental status and alertness
✘ Report Immediately
  • SpO2 dropping despite supplemental O2
  • Increasing tachycardia or hemodynamic instability
  • Hypotension (SBP <90 mmHg)
  • Altered mental status or syncope
  • Signs of active bleeding on anticoagulation
  • Signs of obstructive/cardiogenic shock
  • Respiratory failure (RR >30, accessory muscle use)

Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT)
⚠
The Paradox: HIT = LOW platelets + HIGH clot risk. The drug designed to prevent clots causes life-threatening thrombosis. This is the most dangerous heparin complication and a high-yield exam topic.

Pathophysiology

Heparin binds PF4
→
Antibodies form vs. complex
→
Antibodies activate platelets
→
Massive aggregation
→
Arterial + venous thrombosis
HIT Diagnostic Criteria
  • Onset: 5–10 days after heparin initiation (earlier with prior exposure)
  • Platelet drop to <100,000/mm³ OR ≥50% from baseline
  • New thrombosis despite anticoagulation
  • Skin necrosis at injection sites
HIT Management
  • 🛑 STOP ALL heparin — including flushes and heparin-coated catheters
  • Switch to argatroban (direct thrombin inhibitor) — monitor aPTT
  • ❌ Do NOT switch to warfarin immediately — worsens clotting
  • Transition to warfarin only after platelets >150,000
👩‍⚕️
Nursing Priorities
  • Implement bleeding precautions: soft toothbrush, electric razor, avoid IM injections, minimize venipuncture
  • Assess skin every 4–8 hours for new petechiae or bruising
  • Monitor for internal bleeding: ↓ BP, ↑ HR, ↓ H&H, neuro status changes
  • Avoid aspirin and NSAIDs (impair remaining platelet function)
  • Monitor platelet count daily on any patient receiving heparin
  • Patient education: report any unusual bleeding, bruising, or blood in urine/stool immediately

Anticoagulants
Heparin · Enoxaparin · Warfarin · DOACs · Antidotes — mechanisms, monitoring, nursing priorities
ℹ
Fundamental Distinction: Anticoagulants prevent new clots and prevent existing clots from growing. They do NOT directly dissolve clots (fibrinolytics do that). All anticoagulants carry inherent bleeding risk — assess before every dose.
💉
Heparin (Unfractionated — UFH)
Indirect Thrombin Inhibitor · aPTT Monitoring · IV or SQ
▼
Anticoagulant
MechanismBinds antithrombin → antithrombin inactivates Thrombin (IIa) AND Factor Xa → fibrin production stops → clot formation suppressed. Heparin acts as the "key" that unlocks antithrombin's activity.
RouteIV (continuous infusion) or Subcutaneous — NEVER IM (hematoma risk)
Half-life~1–1.5 hours (short; advantage = effects wear off rapidly if bleeding occurs)
MonitoringaPTT — therapeutic range: 60–80 seconds (1.5–2× normal of ~40 sec) · Check q4–6h during infusion
AntidoteProtamine Sulfate — 1 mg neutralizes 100 units heparin · Max 50 mg in 10 min · Slow IV (too fast = hypotension/anaphylaxis)
Pregnancy Safety✔ SAFE — does not cross placenta. Use instead of warfarin during pregnancy.
UsesMI, DVT/PE treatment, PAD, post-op DVT prophylaxis, PCI anticoagulation bridge

Pre-Administration Assessment

  • Baseline aPTT, CBC (especially platelets), H&H
  • Active bleeding, coagulopathy, severe HTN, peptic ulcer, recent surgery
  • IV heparin must be administered via infusion pump — never by gravity or IV push
  • Know therapeutic range: aPTT 60–80 seconds

Monitoring During Therapy

  • aPTT every 4–6 hours during IV infusion → adjust per protocol
  • Platelet count daily — watch for HIT onset at days 5–10
  • SQ injection: abdomen, 45–90°, do NOT aspirate, apply firm pressure after (do not rub)
  • Signs of bleeding: ↓ BP, ↑ HR, ↓ H&H, bruising, tarry stools, hematuria, severe headache
⚠
HIT Protocol: Platelets <100,000/mm³ OR 50% drop from baseline → STOP ALL heparin (including flushes, coated catheters) → Notify provider → Switch to argatroban if continued anticoagulation required
⚠
Hold Heparin If: aPTT >100 seconds · Active bleeding · Platelets <100,000 · Pre-procedure per protocol

Enoxaparin (Lovenox) — LMWH
Low Molecular Weight Heparin · Factor Xa Inhibitor · SQ Only · Home Use
▼
LMWH Anticoagulant
MechanismActivates antithrombin → preferential inactivation of Factor Xa (with some thrombin inactivation). More predictable than UFH due to consistent pharmacokinetics.
RouteSubcutaneous ONLY · Abdomen preferred · 45-degree angle
Half-life~4–5 hours (longer than UFH → given q12h or q24h)
MonitoringUsually NO routine aPTT monitoring needed (fixed weight-based dosing). Use anti-Xa levels in renal impairment, obesity, or pregnancy.
AntidoteProtamine sulfate (partially reverses — does NOT fully neutralize LMWH)
AdvantagesPredictable dosing · Home self-injection · No aPTT monitoring · Safe in pregnancy
CautionRenally cleared — reduce dose or switch to UFH if GFR <30 mL/min

Key Injection Technique (Different from UFH)

  • ✔ DO expel the air bubble before injection — ensures full dose delivery
  • Do NOT aspirate before injecting
  • Apply gentle pressure after — do NOT rub (increases bruising)
  • Rotate sites — alternate sides of abdomen, minimum 2 inches from navel
⚠
Spinal Anesthesia Risk: Risk of spinal or epidural hematoma with neurological injury. Hold per protocol before spinal procedures or lumbar puncture.

Warfarin (Coumadin)
Vitamin K Antagonist · INR Monitoring · Multiple Drug Interactions
▼
Vitamin K Antagonist
MechanismInhibits hepatic synthesis of Vitamin K–dependent clotting factors: II, VII, IX, X. Must wait 2–5 days for existing factors to deplete before therapeutic effect is achieved.
RouteOral ONLY
OnsetDelayed: 2–5 days to reach therapeutic effect. Warfarin and heparin are overlapped for this reason.
MonitoringINR (International Normalized Ratio)
Therapeutic INR2.0–3.0 for most indications · 2.5–3.5 for mechanical heart valves
AntidoteVitamin K — PO for elective reversal, IV for emergency (onset 6–24h). FFP for immediate reversal. Vitamin K alone requires hours; FFP provides immediate clotting factors.
Pregnancy❌ CONTRAINDICATED — teratogenic. Use heparin throughout pregnancy.

Critical Drug & Food Interactions

⬆
Increases INR (↑ bleed risk):
Aspirin, NSAIDs, antibiotics (kill gut Vit-K bacteria), amiodarone, cimetidine, alcohol
⬇
Decreases INR (↑ clot risk):
Vitamin K–rich foods (spinach, kale, broccoli), rifampin, barbiturates, St. John's Wort

Patient Teaching

  • Take at same time daily — never double-dose if missed
  • Wear medical alert identification indicating anticoagulant use
  • Keep ALL INR appointments — dose adjustments depend on results
  • Maintain consistent Vitamin K intake — do not suddenly increase or eliminate green leafy vegetables
  • Use soft toothbrush, electric razor; avoid aspirin/NSAIDs unless ordered
  • Report: unusual bleeding, bruising, blood in urine/stool, prolonged bleeding from cuts
  • Notify ALL providers (dentist, surgeon) — must be held before procedures
💡
Warfarin NCLEX Focus: The most tested teaching points are: consistent (not zero) Vitamin K intake, INR monitoring compliance, bleeding precautions, and wearing a medical alert ID. "Avoid all green vegetables" is INCORRECT — consistency is the goal.

Antiplatelet Agents
Aspirin · Clopidogrel · Dipyridamole · DAPT · When antiplatelets vs. anticoagulants
ℹ
Antiplatelets vs. Anticoagulants: Antiplatelets prevent Stage 1 hemostasis (platelet plug). Used for arterial thrombosis (MI, stroke, PAD). Anticoagulants interrupt Stage 2 (fibrin). Preferred for venous thrombosis (DVT, PE, Afib). Both carry bleeding risk.

⚠

Absolute Contraindications

⚠
  • Active internal bleeding
  • History of hemorrhagic stroke (any time) OR ischemic stroke within 3 months
  • Intracranial neoplasm, AVM, or aneurysm
  • Recent (<3 months) intracranial/spinal surgery or head trauma
  • Severely uncontrolled hypertension (>180/110 mmHg)
  • Suspected aortic dissection

Nursing During Fibrinolytic Therapy

  • 🚫 NO injections (IM or SQ) during and for hours after infusion
  • 🚫 NO new invasive procedures — avoid new arterial lines, minimize IV starts
  • Apply manual pressure ≥30 minutes to all puncture sites
  • Monitor for ICH q15–30 min: sudden severe headache, confusion, pupil changes, neurological deterioration → STOP infusion immediately → urgent CT head
  • Monitor BP continuously — hypertension elevates ICH risk
  • For STEMI: monitor for reperfusion — pain relief + ST segments returning toward baseline

Women & MI — Atypical Presentation
⚠
Why This Matters: Women are significantly more likely to be misdiagnosed or delayed in treatment during MI because their symptoms differ from the "classic" male presentation. Women have higher in-hospital mortality from MI partly due to delayed recognition. As a nurse, you may be the first to identify that something is wrong.

Classic Presentation (More Common in Men)

  • Crushing, pressure-like chest pain — "elephant on my chest"
  • Chest pain radiating to left arm, jaw, neck, or shoulder
  • Profuse diaphoresis (cold sweat)
  • Sudden onset, severe intensity
  • Clearly identifiable as "cardiac" in nature
  • More likely to present with STEMI pattern

Atypical Presentation (More Common in Women)

⚠
Women may have NO chest pain at all — or only mild, vague discomfort. Their symptoms are frequently attributed to GI issues, anxiety, or fatigue — delaying life-saving treatment.
  • Vague, mild, or absent chest pain
  • More likely to present with NSTEMI
  • Symptoms often occur at rest, not with exertion
  • Symptoms may develop gradually over days

Key Atypical Symptoms in Women — Each Explained

🍽 Indigestion / GI Upset

Why it happens: The inferior wall of the heart sits near the diaphragm. Ischemia in this area activates the vagus nerve → stimulates nausea, epigastric discomfort, and vomiting — mimicking heartburn or GI illness.

How it presents: Upper abdominal pain, burning, bloating, nausea, vomiting — often after eating, leading patient (and providers) to attribute it to a GI cause.

⚠
Nursing pearl: If a woman has new GI symptoms + any of the following — diaphoresis, fatigue, jaw/back pain, or shortness of breath — treat as cardiac until proven otherwise. Obtain ECG immediately.
💓 Palpitations / Heart Fluttering

Why it happens: Ischemic myocardium becomes electrically unstable → triggers arrhythmias, ectopic beats, or accelerated heart rate. Women are also more prone to MINOCA (MI with non-obstructed coronary arteries), which produces dysrhythmias without classic occlusion.

How it presents: "Fluttering," "racing," or "skipping" heartbeats. May come in waves. Can occur without any chest pain. Often dismissed as anxiety, panic disorder, or perimenopause-related palpitations.

ℹ
Nursing pearl: New palpitations in a woman with cardiac risk factors warrants immediate 12-lead ECG. Look for ST changes, new arrhythmias, or premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) in groups.
😴 Unexplained Fatigue

Why it happens: Reduced cardiac output from ischemic myocardium → less blood and oxygen delivered to muscles and brain → profound, unusual, out-of-proportion tiredness. The body's compensatory mechanisms (SNS activation, increased HR) increase overall metabolic demand while supply is compromised.

How it presents: Women often report exhaustion so severe they can't perform usual activities days to weeks before the MI. May describe it as "the worst fatigue of my life" or "I just can't seem to get going." Frequently dismissed as stress, depression, or anemia.

⚠
Nursing pearl: Prodromal fatigue (fatigue occurring days/weeks before the MI) is one of the most reported symptoms in women — reported by up to 70% in some studies. It is a red flag, not a normal finding.
😰 Diaphoresis Without Exertion

Why it happens: SNS activation in response to ischemia → releases catecholamines → triggers cold, clammy sweating. Occurs at rest — not associated with heat or activity — which makes it more clinically significant.

How it presents: Cold sweat, clamminess, or sudden onset sweating for no apparent reason. Can accompany other symptoms or occur in isolation. Often confused with hot flashes in perimenopausal women.

ℹ
Nursing pearl: Cold, clammy diaphoresis at rest in a woman = cardiac emergency until proven otherwise. This reflects SNS activation from ischemia, not thermoregulation.
😮‍💨 Shortness of Breath (Without Chest Pain)

Why it happens: Left ventricular ischemia → impaired contractility → blood backs up into pulmonary circulation → pulmonary congestion → dyspnea. Women are more likely to have microvascular disease, where multiple small vessels are affected rather than a single large occlusion.

How it presents: Unexpected breathlessness at rest or with minimal exertion, new orthopnea (difficulty breathing lying flat), or inability to complete usual activities without becoming winded. Often misattributed to anxiety, asthma, or deconditioning.

🦷 Jaw, Back, or Arm Pain (Atypical Radiation)

Why it happens: Referred pain — the brain receives pain signals from the heart and nearby dermatomal regions and cannot always distinguish the source. In women, pain more often radiates to jaw, back, or both arms rather than the classic left arm.

How it presents: Aching jaw, dental pain, upper back or interscapular pain, or bilateral arm heaviness. May come on suddenly or gradually. Frequently misattributed to musculoskeletal pain or dental problems — leading to dangerous delays.

⚠
Nursing pearl: Jaw pain that is unexplained, new, and associated with other symptoms = cardiac referral until proven otherwise. Interscapular back pain is a classic MI presentation in women.
⚠
Why Women Are Undertreated — The Research Evidence:
Women are less likely to receive aspirin, heparin, beta-blockers, and reperfusion therapy in a timely manner. They wait longer in the ED before receiving an ECG. They are more likely to be told their symptoms are anxiety or GI-related. As the nurse, you are the patient's advocate — if a woman with cardiac risk factors presents with these symptoms, escalate care and document your clinical concerns.

Risk Factors Unique to or Amplified in Women

  • Pregnancy complications — pre-eclampsia, gestational diabetes, preterm birth → long-term CV risk marker
  • Oral contraceptives — especially combined with smoking → dramatically ↑ thrombosis risk
  • Hormonal changes — estrogen loss after menopause removes cardioprotective effect; risk rises sharply post-menopause
  • Autoimmune conditions (lupus, rheumatoid arthritis) — more prevalent in women; accelerate atherosclerosis
  • Depression and psychological stress — stronger predictor of MI in women than in men
  • MINOCA (MI with non-obstructive coronary arteries) — more common in women; caused by coronary spasm, plaque erosion, microvascular disease — classic angiography may appear normal
  • Younger women with MI — frequently have fewer "traditional" risk factors; often go unrecognized
  • Diabetes — eliminates the hormonal protective effect; doubles CV risk in women vs. 1.5× in men

⚠
Definition: Cardiogenic shock is a state of critical end-organ hypoperfusion caused by primary cardiac failure. It is the most lethal form of shock, with mortality rates of 40–60%. Most commonly occurs as a complication of STEMI when >40% of the left ventricular myocardium is necrotic or ischemic.

The Vicious Cycle: The compensatory mechanisms that try to maintain BP (SNS activation, tachycardia, vasoconstriction) actually worsen myocardial oxygen demand → further ischemia → further pump failure. Breaking this cycle requires early reperfusion and hemodynamic support.

Causes of Cardiogenic Shock

Most Common — LV Failure
  • STEMI (most common cause — >80% of cases)
  • Large anterior MI (LAD territory) — worst prognosis
  • Acute decompensated HF
  • Myocarditis (inflammatory heart disease)
Mechanical Complications
  • Ventricular septal rupture (post-MI)
  • Acute mitral regurgitation (papillary muscle rupture)
  • Free wall rupture (rare, often fatal)
  • Cardiac tamponade
Right Ventricular Shock
  • Right ventricular MI (from right coronary occlusion)
  • Massive pulmonary embolism
  • RV failure from severe pulmonary HTN
  • Preload-dependent — IV fluids are treatment

Clinical Presentation — Nursing Assessment

Cardiovascular Findings

  • Hypotension (SBP <90 or MAP <65 mmHg)
  • Tachycardia (compensatory — HR often 100–130)
  • Narrow pulse pressure (systolic – diastolic <25)
  • Distant, muffled heart sounds
  • S3 gallop — hallmark of systolic dysfunction
  • Murmurs — new or changed (mechanical complication)
  • JVD — elevated venous pressure
  • Weak, thready peripheral pulses
  • Prolonged capillary refill (>3 seconds)

Pulmonary Findings

  • Bilateral crackles (rales) — pulmonary edema
  • Tachypnea (RR >22)
  • Frothy, pink-tinged sputum (severe pulmonary edema)
  • SpO2 declining despite supplemental O2
  • Use of accessory muscles; orthopnea

Renal Findings

  • Oliguria (<0.5 mL/kg/hr) → anuria
  • Rising BUN and creatinine (pre-renal → intrinsic AKI)
  • Concentrated urine (dark, high specific gravity)
  • Electrolyte imbalances develop rapidly

Neurological / Skin Findings

  • Agitation, restlessness (earliest neuro sign)
  • Confusion, disorientation
  • Obtundation or unresponsiveness (severe)
  • Cold, pale, clammy skin
  • Mottling (livedo reticularis) — blue-purple lacy pattern on skin
  • Cyanosis of lips and fingertips

Lab Findings

  • ↑ Troponin (myocardial necrosis ongoing)
  • ↑ BNP (LV wall stress)
  • Metabolic acidosis (↑ lactate, ↓ pH, ↓ HCO3)
  • ↑ BUN/creatinine (renal hypoperfusion)
  • ↑ LFTs (hepatic hypoperfusion — "shock liver")
  • Coagulopathy — DIC may develop in severe cases

Treatment — Priority-Ordered Management

1. Immediate Reperfusion
  • Emergency PCI is the priority — door-to-balloon ≤90 min
  • Opens occluded coronary artery → restores myocardial blood flow → the only intervention that addresses the underlying cause
  • CABG if multivessel disease not amenable to PCI
  • All other treatments are temporizing until reperfusion
2. Hemodynamic Support
  • Dobutamine — positive inotrope (β1 agonist); ↑ CO without major ↑ HR; FIRST-LINE for cardiogenic shock
  • Norepinephrine — vasopressor if MAP <65 despite dobutamine; ↑ SVR to maintain perfusion pressure
  • Dopamine — alternative to norepinephrine (higher arrhythmia risk)
  • Avoid aggressive fluid boluses (↑ preload worsens pulmonary edema in LV failure)
3. Mechanical Circulatory Support
  • IABP (Intra-Aortic Balloon Pump) — inflates in diastole (↑ coronary perfusion), deflates in systole (↓ afterload); requires arterial line; monitor for limb ischemia
  • Impella — microaxial pump across aortic valve; directly unloads LV
  • ECMO — extracorporeal membrane oxygenation; full cardiopulmonary bypass support for refractory shock
Respiratory Support
  • High-flow oxygen or non-invasive ventilation (BiPAP) for pulmonary edema
  • Intubation and mechanical ventilation if patient cannot protect airway or is severely hypoxic
  • Furosemide IV — reduces pulmonary congestion if BP permits (SBP >90)
  • Target SpO2 ≥94%, PaO2 ≥60 mmHg
RV Shock — Critical Difference
  • Right ventricular MI → RV cannot fill pulmonary circulation → ↓ LV preload → ↓ CO
  • PRELOAD DEPENDENT — requires IV fluid boluses (opposite of LV shock)
  • Avoid nitroglycerin and diuretics (reduce preload → catastrophic ↓ CO)
  • Maintain HR 60–100; AV pacing if bradycardia
  • Signs: JVD + clear lungs + hypotension (Kussmaul's triad)

Primary vs. Secondary HTN

  • Develops gradually over years
  • Managed with lifestyle changes + medications

  • Hyperthyroidism, Cushing's syndrome, pheochromocytoma
  • Sleep apnea, medications (OCP, NSAIDs, decongestants)

BP Regulation Mechanisms
Sympathetic Nervous System (SNS)
  • SNS activation → releases norepinephrine
  • ↑ HR + ↑ contractility (β1 receptors)
  • Vasoconstriction (α1 receptors)
  • ↑ Renin release from kidneys
  • Net effect: ↑ CO + ↑ SVR → ↑ BP
  • Beta-blockers and α-blockers target this
RAAS (Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone)
  • ↓ BP or ↓ Na → kidney releases renin
  • Renin → converts angiotensinogen → Ang I
  • ACE converts Ang I → Ang II
  • Ang II → vasoconstriction + aldosterone
  • Aldosterone → Na/H2O retention → ↑ preload
  • ACEIs, ARBs, spironolactone target this
Baroreceptor Reflex
  • Baroreceptors in aortic arch and carotid sinus
  • Detect stretch from elevated BP
  • Signal medulla → reduce SNS output
  • ↓ HR + vasodilation → ↓ BP
  • Short-term buffer; resets in chronic HTN
  • Explains reflex tachycardia with vasodilators

  • High sodium diet
  • Obesity / sedentary lifestyle
  • Tobacco use
  • Excessive alcohol
  • Stress / psychological factors
  • Medications (NSAIDs, OCP, decongestants, stimulants)

  • Age (risk increases >55 men, >65 women)
  • Family history / genetic predisposition
  • Race (higher prevalence and severity in Black Americans)
  • Chronic kidney disease
  • Diabetes mellitus

Hypertensive Crisis
Emergency vs. Urgency · Pathophysiology · Symptoms · Medications · Nursing management
⚠
Definition: BP ≥180/120 mmHg. Requires immediate assessment to determine if end-organ damage is present (Emergency) or absent (Urgency). The degree of BP elevation matters less than the presence of target organ damage.
🚨
Hypertensive Emergency
⚠
BP ≥180/120 WITH acute end-organ damage

Signs of Target Organ Damage

  • 🧠 Neurological: Encephalopathy, altered mental status, stroke, seizures, severe headache, visual changes, papilledema
  • ❤️ Cardiac: Acute MI, unstable angina, acute HF, aortic dissection
  • 🫁 Pulmonary: Acute pulmonary edema, dyspnea at rest
  • 🫘 Renal: Acute kidney injury — rising creatinine, hematuria, oliguria
  • 🤰 Obstetric: Eclampsia (seizures in pregnancy with HTN)

Treatment Goal

⚠
Reduce MAP by NO MORE than 25% in the first hour, then to 160/100 over next 2–6 hours. Too-rapid reduction causes ischemia (cerebral, coronary, renal) — organs have autoregulated to high BP.
  • IV antihypertensives in ICU setting
  • Continuous BP monitoring (arterial line often used)
  • Frequent neuro assessments
⚡
Hypertensive Urgency
ℹ
BP ≥180/120 WITHOUT acute end-organ damage

Characteristics

  • Severe BP elevation but no acute organ compromise
  • May have mild headache or epistaxis
  • Often from medication non-adherence
  • Can be managed in outpatient or ED setting

Treatment Goal

  • Reduce BP gradually over 24–48 hours
  • Oral antihypertensives preferred
  • Resume/adjust home medications
  • Reassess in 24–72 hours
  • Address contributing factors (pain, anxiety, non-compliance)
💡
Common triggers: Missed doses, pain, anxiety, stimulant use, rebound from clonidine discontinuation

Peripheral Arterial Disease (PAD)
Atherosclerosis · Pathophysiology · ABI · Intermittent claudication · Medications · Acute ischemia
📖
Definition & Pathophysiology

PAD is the progressive narrowing of peripheral arteries — most commonly the lower extremities — due to atherosclerosis. Reduced arterial blood flow causes ischemia, pain, and eventually tissue loss.

Atherosclerotic plaque accumulates in peripheral artery
→
Lumen narrows → ↓ blood flow
→
Ischemia → claudication → rest pain → ulceration → gangrene

Most Affected Arteries

  • Aortoiliac (Leriche syndrome — bilateral claudication + impotence)
  • Femoral-popliteal (most common)
  • Tibial-peroneal (distal — worst prognosis)

Risk Factors

  • Smoking — #1 modifiable risk factor
  • Diabetes mellitus (greatly accelerates atherosclerosis)
  • Hypertension
  • Hyperlipidemia
  • Age >50, male sex, family history
  • CKD, inflammatory conditions

ℹ

Venous Insufficiency — Pathophysiology

Chronic venous insufficiency (CVI) occurs when venous valves become incompetent, allowing blood to pool in the lower extremities rather than returning efficiently to the heart. This leads to venous hypertension, edema, skin changes, and ulceration.

Incompetent venous valves
→
Blood reflux → pools in lower legs
→
Venous hypertension
→
Fluid leak → edema + hemosiderin deposits
→
Stasis ulcers (medial ankle)

Risk Factors

  • History of DVT (post-thrombotic syndrome)
  • Prolonged standing or sitting
  • Obesity
  • Female sex, multiparity
  • Advanced age
  • Varicose veins
  • Sedentary lifestyle

Signs & Symptoms

  • Reddish-brown skin discoloration — hemosiderin staining (iron deposits from RBC breakdown)
  • Pitting edema — worsens with standing, improves with elevation
  • Aching, heaviness, fatigue in legs
  • Varicose veins (dilated, tortuous superficial veins)
  • Lipodermatosclerosis (fibrotic, hardened skin)
  • Venous stasis ulcers (medial ankle — characteristic location)
  • Pruritus over affected area

Venous Stasis Ulcers — Management

Wound Characteristics

  • Location: medial ankle / gaiter area
  • Irregular, sloping edges
  • Ruddy, granulating wound base
  • Moderate to heavy exudate
  • Surrounding hemosiderin staining
  • Moderate pain (less than arterial ulcers)
  • Surrounding skin may be indurated or weepy

Wound Care Principles

  • Moist wound healing environment (moisture-retentive dressings)
  • Debridement as needed (enzymatic or sharp per provider order)
  • Manage exudate: absorptive dressings (alginate, foam)
  • Protect periwound skin with barrier products
  • Do NOT use compression in arterial wounds — assess ABI first

Compression Therapy — Key Treatment

ℹ
Compression is the cornerstone of venous ulcer treatment. It counteracts venous hypertension by providing external counter-pressure.
  • Compression stockings: 30–40 mmHg graduated compression — applied upon waking before legs become edematous
  • Multi-layer compression bandaging: Unna's boot, 4-layer bandage systems
  • Unna's Boot: Zinc-oxide impregnated gauze; moist, medicated; changed weekly; promotes healing in ambulatory patients
  • Pneumatic compression devices for non-ambulatory patients
  • NEVER apply compression to ABI <0.6 — arterial compromise will worsen

Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT)
⚠
DVT is a life-threatening vascular emergency due to risk of PE. A thrombus in a deep leg vein can dislodge, travel through the venous system, and cause pulmonary embolism. DVT prevention is a key nursing priority for all hospitalized patients.

Signs & Symptoms

  • Unilateral leg swelling — most common
  • Calf/leg pain or tenderness
  • Warmth and redness over affected area
  • Dilated superficial veins
  • Low-grade fever
  • Homans' sign (calf pain with dorsiflexion) — poor sensitivity/specificity, NOT reliable; do NOT elicit forcefully (may dislodge clot)
  • Many DVTs are asymptomatic — detected via surveillance

Diagnosis & Treatment

  • Venous duplex ultrasound — gold standard for DVT diagnosis; non-invasive; first-line
  • D-dimer — elevated; highly sensitive; negative rules out DVT in low-risk patients
  • Treatment: Anticoagulation (UFH, LMWH, or DOACs) for minimum 3 months
  • DVT in leg without PE: DOACs (apixaban, rivaroxaban) preferred for outpatient treatment
  • Ambulation encouraged (with anticoagulation) — rest does NOT reduce PE risk
  • Compression stockings for post-thrombotic syndrome prevention

DVT Prevention — Every Hospitalized Patient

Mechanical
  • Sequential compression devices (SCDs) — on all non-ambulatory patients
  • Compression stockings
  • Early ambulation — most effective non-pharmacologic prevention
Pharmacologic
  • Enoxaparin (LMWH) — most common prophylaxis
  • Low-dose UFH (heparin 5,000 units SQ BID/TID)
  • DOACs for high-risk orthopedic patients
Nursing Actions
  • Ensure SCDs are applied and functioning per shift
  • Encourage and assist with ambulation ASAP
  • Leg exercises in bed if immobile (ankle pumps, quad sets)
  • Adequate hydration

Clinical Pearls & Exam Preparation
High-yield NCLEX traps · Lab reference · Complete drug comparison · Antidotes · Clinical reasoning
⚠
High-Yield Exam Traps
⚠ HIT Paradox
HIT = LOW platelets + HIGH clot risk. Heparin causes the very thing it prevents. Daily platelet count on heparin is mandatory. Platelets <100,000 or 50% drop → stop ALL heparin → argatroban.
⚠ Digoxin + Diuretics = Toxicity
Loop/thiazide diuretics → hypokalemia → sensitizes heart to digoxin → toxicity at normal drug levels. Always check K+ before digoxin. Hold if K+ <3.5 mEq/L. This combination requires vigilant monitoring.
⚠ NTG + Sildenafil = Fatal Hypotension
NEVER combine nitroglycerin with PDE5 inhibitors (sildenafil/Viagra, tadalafil/Cialis, vardenafil). Both cause vasodilation → profound, potentially fatal hypotension. Ask before every NTG dose.
⚠ Warfarin Diet Teaching
Teach CONSISTENT Vitamin K intake — NOT zero. Sudden increases or decreases destabilize INR unpredictably. "Avoid all green vegetables" is incorrect. Consistency is the clinical goal.
⚠ STEMI vs. NSTEMI Fibrinolytics
Alteplase/tPA = STEMI ONLY (ST elevation, complete occlusion). NSTEMI has partial occlusion → fibrinolytics are NOT indicated and can cause harm. ECG is the differentiating factor.
⚠ Aspirin — Chewed for MI
First dose for suspected MI: 162–325 mg CHEWED (not swallowed) for rapid buccal absorption. This is a consistently tested nursing priority action. Maintenance post-MI: 81 mg/day indefinitely.
⚠ ACEI Cough vs. Angioedema
Dry cough = bradykinin effect → switch to ARB (benign, manageable). Angioedema (tongue/throat swelling) = EMERGENCY → stop drug + rapid response + epinephrine. Two entirely different clinical outcomes.
⚠ Never Stop Beta-Blockers Abruptly
Rebound tachycardia and hypertension on abrupt discontinuation can precipitate fatal MI. Must taper gradually. Even pre-procedure — coordinate with provider. This applies to all beta-blockers.
⚠ Loop vs. Thiazide Electrolytes
Loop diuretics → HYPOCALCEMIA. Thiazide diuretics → HYPERCALCEMIA. Both cause hypokalemia, hyponatremia, hypomagnesemia, hyperuricemia. This distinction is a repeated NCLEX test point.
⚠ Spironolactone + ACEI = Hyperkalemia
Both retain K+ by different mechanisms → combined effect can cause severe, fatal hyperkalemia. Monitor K+ closely. Hold spironolactone if K+ >5.0 mEq/L. Watch for peaked T waves on ECG.
⚠ Heparin IV Must Use Pump
IV heparin must be administered via infusion pump — never by gravity drip or IV push. Enoxaparin: SQ at 45°, no aspiration, DO expel air bubble (opposite of insulin technique).
⚠ Cardiogenic Shock Recognition
UO <0.5 mL/kg/hr + SBP <90 + cold clammy skin + tachycardia = cardiogenic shock. Report immediately. Dobutamine is the drug of choice. RV necrosis >40% → extremely high mortality.
⚠ Left vs. Right HF Signs
Left HF = PULMONARY: bibasilar crackles, orthopnea, PND, frothy pink sputum, S3 gallop. Right HF = PERIPHERAL: JVD, pitting edema, ascites, hepatomegaly, weight gain. Memorize both sides.
⚠ Protamine Administration Speed
Slow IV — maximum 50 mg in 10 minutes. Rapid administration causes hypotension and anaphylaxis. 1 mg protamine neutralizes 100 units heparin. Have emergency equipment available at bedside.
⚠ Beta-Blockers Mask Hypoglycemia
Beta-blockers block tachycardia and palpitations — warning signs of hypoglycemia. Only diaphoresis is preserved. Requires extra blood glucose monitoring in diabetic patients on beta-blockers.
