Normal Cardiovascular & Perfusion PhysiologyFoundation — Know What's Normal Before Identifying What's Wrong

Functions of the Heart & Circulatory Systems

The cardiovascular system exists for one essential purpose: perfusion — delivering oxygenated blood to every tissue in the body and returning deoxygenated blood for gas exchange. This is accomplished by three interdependent systems working in concert.

Coronary circulation — the network of vessels that supply the heart muscle (myocardium) itself. When coronary perfusion fails, the muscle that pumps blood for the entire body becomes ischemic.
Pulmonary circulation — carries deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle to the lungs for gas exchange (intake of O₂, release of CO₂), then returns oxygenated blood to the left atrium. Requires intact ventilation (air movement), respiration (alveolar–capillary gas exchange), and perfusion (blood passing through pulmonary capillaries).
Systemic circulation — the left ventricle ejects oxygenated blood into the aorta; arteries distribute it to tissues; veins return deoxygenated blood to the right atrium. The circuit then repeats.

Cardiac Output & Performance

CO = SV × HR
Cardiac Output (4–8 L/min) = Stroke Volume (~70 mL/beat) × Heart Rate (60–100 bpm)
💡

The Single Most Important Therapeutic Principle

The goal of medication therapy with any cardiac patient is to DECREASE cardiac workload by manipulating SV, HR, preload, afterload, and/or contractility. Every perfusion drug you learn works by reducing one or more of these factors to lighten the burden on a struggling heart.

Cardiac cycle — one complete heartbeat consisting of systole (contraction/ejection) followed by diastole (relaxation/filling).
Stroke volume (SV) — the volume of blood ejected from a ventricle with each contraction (~70 mL). SV is influenced by the amount of blood returning to the right atrium from venous circulation and to the left atrium from pulmonary veins. A higher SV increases workload of the heart (typically due to excess volume). Determined by three factors: preload, afterload, and contractility.
🚩

Why Tachycardia Hurts Cardiac Output

Tachycardia increases myocardial oxygen demand and workload, but it actually decreases stroke volume because the ventricles have less time to fill during the shortened diastole. So while the heart is beating faster and working harder, each beat ejects less blood — a vicious cycle that worsens perfusion, especially in patients with existing heart disease.

Ejection fraction (EF) — the percentage of blood ejected from the ventricle during systole, measured on echocardiogram. Normal = 60–70%. As EF drops, it reflects progressive loss of cardiac contractility and worsening heart failure.

The Four Determinants of Cardiac Output

Regulation of Heart Rate & Blood Pressure

Neurological Regulation

The autonomic nervous system (ANS) provides moment-to-moment control. Sympathetic stimulation releases norepinephrine and epinephrine, increasing HR, contractility, and vasoconstriction (↑ CO and ↑ BP). Parasympathetic (vagal) input slows HR.

Baroreceptors

Stretch receptors in the aortic arch and carotid sinus sense arterial pressure. When BP is high, they signal the vasomotor center in the medulla to promote vasodilation (↓ afterload). When BP drops, the medulla triggers vasoconstriction (↑ afterload).

Hormonal Regulation — The RAAS

The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) is the body's primary long-term compensatory mechanism for maintaining BP and renal perfusion:

Decreased renal perfusion → juxtaglomerular cells release renin → renin converts angiotensinogen to angiotensin I → ACE converts angiotensin I to angiotensin II (a potent vasoconstrictor) → angiotensin II stimulates aldosterone release → aldosterone promotes Na⁺ and water retention → ↑ blood volume (↑ preload) and ↑ vascular resistance (↑ afterload) → ↑ BP.

💡

Clinical Pearl — Why RAAS Matters Everywhere

RAAS is the physiologic rationale behind ACE inhibitors, ARBs, aldosterone antagonists, and beta blockers (which reduce renin release). Understanding this single pathway explains the mechanism of nearly half the drugs on the perfusion exam.

Frank-Starling Law of the Heart

"The greater the blood volume (preload) entering the ventricle during diastole, the greater the volume ejected during systole — up to a physiological limit, beyond which the overstretched muscle loses contractile force."

The greatest force of contraction occurs when cardiac muscle fibers are stretched to approximately 2.5 times their resting length. Beyond this point, further stretching actually decreases contractility — like an overstretched rubber band that loses its snap. This is exactly what happens in chronic heart failure: the dilated, overstretched ventricle becomes increasingly inefficient. This is why an enlarged heart on imaging is a bad sign, not a sign of strength.

♥ High-Yield Takeaways — Normal Physiology
  • CO = SV × HR; anything that alters preload, afterload, contractility, or HR can change perfusion status.
  • EF 60–70% is normal; below 40% = systolic HF with significantly impaired perfusion.
  • RAAS is compensatory short-term but pathological long-term — it is the target of ACEIs, ARBs, aldosterone blockers, and beta blockers.
  • Frank-Starling law: stretching helps contractility up to a point, then the heart decompensates — this is the transition from compensated to decompensated HF.

AtherosclerosisThe Root Cause of Most Cardiovascular Disease

Definitions & Distinction

Arteriosclerosis — a general term for thickening and hardening of the arterial wall, often age-related.
Atherosclerosis — a specific type of arteriosclerosis involving plaque formation within the arterial wall. It is the leading risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD) and affects large arteries: coronary, aorta, carotid, vertebral, renal, iliac, and femoral beds.

Risk Factors

Modifiable

  • Smoking & tobacco use
  • Hypertension
  • Hyperlipidemia (↑ LDL-C, ↑ triglycerides)
  • Diabetes mellitus
  • Obesity / overweight
  • Physical inactivity
  • Unhealthy diet
  • Excessive alcohol intake
  • Chronic inflammatory conditions
  • Mental / psychosocial stress

Non-Modifiable

  • Age: Men > 45; Women > 55
  • Family history: Premature CVD in first-degree relative (men < 55, women < 65)
  • Genetics: Inherited predispositions (e.g., familial hypercholesterolemia)

Pathophysiology — Cause-and-Effect Sequence

The progression of atherosclerosis follows a predictable inflammatory cascade:

🔬

Step-by-Step Disease Progression

Step 1 — Endothelial injury. The process begins when the inner lining of the blood vessel is damaged. Triggers include smoking, hypertension, diabetes (which elevates LDL-C and triglycerides), and chemical injury from elevated LDL-C or decreased HDL-C.

Step 2 — Inflammation and fatty-streak formation. The damaged endothelium attracts monocytes and lipids. Macrophages engulf LDL cholesterol, becoming foam cells that form fatty streaks along the vessel intima.

Step 3 — Fibrous plaque development. Collagen and smooth muscle proliferate over the fatty streak, forming a fibrous cap. The plaque narrows the vessel lumen, reducing blood flow.

Step 4 — Advanced or complicated lesions. Plaques become calcified, hemorrhagic, ulcerated, or thrombosed, affecting all layers of the vessel wall.

Step 5 — Acute event. Plaque rupture exposes subendothelial tissue, triggering rapid platelet adhesion and thrombus formation. The resulting sudden vessel occlusion causes ischemia and infarction — this is the mechanism behind MI, stroke, and acute limb ischemia.

🚩

Red Flag — The Lipid Link

Elevated LDL-C ("bad cholesterol") and decreased HDL-C ("good cholesterol") directly cause chemical injury to the vessel wall and accelerate plaque formation. Diabetes further worsens this by promoting ↑ LDL-C and ↑ triglycerides. This is the pathophysiologic rationale for statin therapy.

Pharmacology — Atherosclerosis

Statins — HMG-CoA Reductase Inhibitors

💊

atorvastatin (Lipitor) — Prototype

Mechanism of Action

Inhibits HMG-CoA reductase, the rate-limiting enzyme in cholesterol synthesis, and decreases apolipoprotein B-100 production. With less cholesterol being manufactured in the liver, the liver compensates by upregulating LDL receptors on its surface, which pulls more LDL-C out of the bloodstream — effectively lowering circulating cholesterol levels.

Indications

Hypercholesterolemia; risk reduction for MI, angina, and stroke.

Adverse Effects

Generally well tolerated. Common side effects include headache, memory changes, and GI upset. The most serious adverse effect is rhabdomyolysis, a rare but life-threatening breakdown of skeletal muscle tissue that releases myoglobin into the blood and can cause kidney damage.

Nursing Considerations

If discontinued, lipid levels return to baseline — this is a lifelong medication. Statins are ineffective in patients genetically unable to synthesize LDL receptors (homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia). Teach the patient to report unexplained muscle pain, tenderness, or weakness and tea-colored urine — these are signs of rhabdomyolysis. Monitor liver function tests and lipid panels regularly.

Non-Pharmacologic Adjuncts

Low-fat diet, regular exercise, and tobacco cessation. Others in class include lovastatin, simvastatin, and pitavastatin.

Expected Outcome

Lowered LDL-C levels on fasting lipid panel; reduced cardiovascular risk.

Antiplatelet Agents

💊

aspirin (ASA)

Mechanism of Action

Irreversibly inhibits COX-1 in platelets, which blocks thromboxane A₂ production. Without thromboxane A₂, platelets cannot aggregate effectively, which prevents arterial thrombus formation. Because the inhibition is irreversible, the antiplatelet effect lasts the entire lifespan of the platelet (about 7–10 days).

Indications

Suppression of platelet aggregation; secondary prevention of MI, stroke, and PAD complications.

Adverse Effects

Bleeding is the primary concern. GI ulceration can lead to significant GI bleeding, so H. pylori testing is recommended before long-term therapy and a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) should be considered for GI protection. Aspirin is contraindicated in patients with bleeding disorders.

Nursing Considerations

Discontinue high-dose ASA one week prior to surgical procedures. Monitor renal function. Teach the patient to recognize signs of bleeding: blood in urine, black tarry stools, coffee-ground emesis, and tinnitus. Use cautiously with any other agents that increase bleeding risk. Drug-drug interactions include other anticoagulants, antiplatelet agents, and NSAIDs.

Expected Outcome

No signs of MI, PAD complications, or stroke.

💊

clopidogrel (Plavix)

Mechanism of Action

Blocks P2Y12 ADP receptors on platelets, which prevents ADP-stimulated platelet aggregation. This is a different antiplatelet pathway than aspirin, which is why the two drugs are often used together (dual antiplatelet therapy) — they complement each other by blocking two separate triggers for clot formation.

Indications

Coronary artery stents (to prevent stent thrombosis), MI, ischemic stroke, and PAD.

Adverse Effects

GI symptoms, bleeding, and bruising are the main concerns.

Nursing Considerations

Discontinue 5 days prior to surgery (compared to aspirin's 7-day window). Teach the same bleeding signs as aspirin: blood in urine, black tarry stools, coffee-ground emesis. Avoid combining with other drugs that increase bleeding risk without provider guidance.

Expected Outcome

Prevention of thrombotic events.

Anticoagulant

💊

heparin (unfractionated)

Mechanism of Action

Binds to antithrombin, which dramatically accelerates the inactivation of clotting factor Xa and thrombin. This suppresses the coagulation cascade. Important: heparin does NOT dissolve existing clots — it prevents clot extension and the formation of new clots.

Indications

MI, PAD, PE, DVT, and post-operative DVT prophylaxis.

Adverse Effects

Hemorrhage is the primary concern. Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is a serious immune-mediated complication where platelet counts drop significantly, paradoxically increasing thrombosis risk.

Nursing Considerations

Contraindicated in active bleeding disorders. Monitor aPTT every 4–6 hours — the therapeutic goal is 1.5–2× normal (normal aPTT is approximately 40 seconds, so therapeutic range is 60–80 seconds). Administer SubQ or IV only — never IM. Monitor closely for signs of bleeding: decreased BP, tachycardia, bruises, petechiae, hematomas, red or black tarry stool, discolored urine, pelvic pain, headache, and lumbar pain. Discontinue immediately if significant bleeding develops. Antidote: protamine sulfate.

Expected Outcome

Prevention of thrombosis without hemorrhagic complications.

♥ High-Yield Takeaways — Atherosclerosis
  • Atherosclerosis = inflammation + lipid deposition → plaque → rupture → thrombosis → ischemia/infarction.
  • Statins ↓ cholesterol production; lifelong therapy; watch for rhabdomyolysis (tea-colored urine, muscle pain).
  • Aspirin (COX-1) and clopidogrel (P2Y12) block platelets via different pathways — know the distinct drug-drug and pre-surgical timelines.
  • Heparin → monitor aPTT (60–80 sec therapeutic); antidote = protamine sulfate.
  • All anti-clotting agents share one universal nursing priority: assess for and teach signs of bleeding.

HypertensionThe "Silent Killer" — Pathophysiology & Pharmacologic Management

Definition & Significance

Hypertension (HTN) is persistently elevated systemic blood pressure. The AHA goal is BP < 130/80 mmHg for all adults. HTN is called the "silent killer" because it is often asymptomatic until it causes end-organ damage: stroke, MI, heart failure, chronic kidney disease, and retinopathy.

BP = CO × PVR
Blood Pressure = Cardiac Output × Peripheral Vascular Resistance
Therefore: any factor that ↑ CO (via ↑ SV or ↑ HR) or ↑ PVR can ↑ BP and impair perfusion.

Pathophysiology — How HTN Develops

Blood pressure is maintained by the interplay of the ANS, circulating catecholamines (NE, epi), the RAAS, baroreceptors, and the vasomotor center. In chronic HTN, these regulatory systems become maladaptive:

🔬

Mechanism of Sustained HTN

RAAS over-activation is the central driver. Excessive angiotensin II causes persistent vasoconstriction, increasing afterload. Aldosterone promotes chronic sodium and water retention, increasing preload and circulating volume. Over time, the elevated pressures damage vessel walls, trigger arteriosclerosis, and create a self-perpetuating cycle of increasing resistance.

Baroreceptor resetting compounds the problem. Chronically high pressures "reset" the baroreceptors to accept the new elevated baseline as normal, so the reflex that should correct high BP no longer kicks in.

Vasomotor center dysfunction maintains the cycle. Sustained sympathetic tone keeps arteries constricted even when the body needs them to relax.

Pharmacology — Antihypertensive Classes

All HTN drugs work by reducing one or more components of the BP equation: CO (via ↓ HR, ↓ SV, or ↓ volume) or PVR (via vasodilation). Understanding where each class acts is the key to mastering this content.

Loop Diuretics

💊

furosemide (Lasix) & bumetanide (Bumex) — Most Potent Diuretics

Mechanism of Action

Blocks the Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻ cotransporter (chloride pump) in the ascending loop of Henle, preventing reabsorption of Na⁺, Cl⁻, and water. This produces rapid, large-volume diuresis — which is why these are called "high-ceiling" diuretics. They produce greater diuresis than any other class. ↓ PRELOAD ↓ VOLUME. Because of their potency, loops are not typically used for maintenance — they are the go-to when rapid, aggressive diuresis is needed, such as in acute HF exacerbation.

Indications

HTN not controlled with milder diuretics; HF (edema); acute pulmonary edema.

Adverse Effects & Lab Changes

Electrolyte losses are the primary concern: hypokalemia (watch for inverted T waves on ECG), hyponatremia, hypocalcemia, hypomagnesemia, and hypochloremia. Metabolic effects include hyperglycemia (use caution in DM), hyperuricemia, elevated BUN/creatinine, and elevated lipids. Other adverse effects include hypotension with dizziness, dehydration (dry mouth, thirst, oliguria), and ototoxicity.

Critical Drug Interaction

Digoxin + loop-induced hypokalemia → digoxin toxicity (bradycardia, N/V, visual disturbances). This is one of the most commonly tested drug interactions in nursing pharmacology.

Nursing Considerations

Obtain BP before administering — hold if SBP < 90. Monitor I&O, daily weights, lung sounds, and electrolytes (especially K⁺, Na⁺, Mg²⁺). Monitor GFR and creatinine. Withhold and notify the provider for significant electrolyte imbalances or hypotension. Furosemide and bumetanide are sulfonamides, so always assess for sulfa allergy. Never combine two loop diuretics together. Change positions slowly in elderly patients to prevent orthostatic changes. Loop diuretics may also increase anticoagulation effects.

Expected Outcome

BP ≤ 130/80; reduced edema (documented by daily weight); increased UOP.

Thiazide Diuretics

💊

hydrochlorothiazide (HCTZ)

Mechanism of Action

Blocks Na⁺/Cl⁻ reabsorption in the distal convoluted tubule, producing moderate diuresis ↓ PRELOAD ↓ VOLUME. Thiazides produce less dramatic diuresis than loops, making them the most common maintenance diuretic for ongoing blood pressure management.

Indications

First-line HTN; HF (especially in older adults).

Adverse Effects

Similar electrolyte profile to loops with one critical difference: thiazides cause hypercalcemia (loops cause hypocalcemia). Also expect hypokalemia, hyponatremia, hypochloremia, hyperuricemia, hyperglycemia, and hyperlipidemia.

Nursing Considerations

Same digoxin toxicity risk via hypokalemia. Monitor electrolytes, I&O, and daily weight. Withhold and notify the provider for electrolyte abnormalities or hypotension.

Expected Outcome

BP ≤ 130/80; reduced edema; increased UOP.

💡

Exam Pearl — Loop vs. Thiazide Calcium Effect

Loops → hypocalcemia (calcium is Lost in the Loop). Thiazides → hypercalcemia (Thiazides hoard — they retain calcium). Both cause hypokalemia. This distinction is frequently tested.

Potassium-Sparing Diuretics

💊

spironolactone (Aldactone) — Prototype

Mechanism of Action

Blocks aldosterone receptors in the distal nephron, which promotes Na⁺ and water excretion while retaining K⁺ ↓ PRELOAD ↓ VOLUME. This is the weakest class of diuretics because it does not directly block sodium reabsorption. It is used primarily when the patient is at risk for developing hypokalemia, not when aggressive diuresis is needed.

Indications

HTN, edema from HF, counteracting K⁺ loss from loop or thiazide diuretics, and lowering digoxin toxicity risk.

Adverse Effects

Hyperkalemia is the primary concern — watch for lethargy, confusion, muscle cramps, ECG changes, and arrhythmias. Gynecomastia (breast tissue growth) is an endocrine side effect unique to this drug.

Nursing Considerations

Monitor serum K⁺ closely — withhold if K⁺ > 5 mEq/L. Patients must avoid K⁺ supplements and salt substitutes (which contain KCl). Do NOT combine with ACEIs or ARBs because all three raise K⁺, creating a risk of life-threatening hyperkalemia. If ACEI or ARB therapy is started, discontinue the K⁺-sparing diuretic first; it may be resumed later if needed. Spironolactone can be given alongside a loop or thiazide to balance K⁺ levels — this is a common combination. Monitor UOP and report output < 30 mL/hr or < 600 mL/day.

Expected Outcome

BP ≤ 130/80; reduced edema; balanced K⁺ levels.

Combination Diuretics (Know These Names)

Combining a K⁺-sparing agent with a loop or thiazide intensifies diuresis while preventing K⁺ loss:

Aldactazide = spironolactone + HCTZ  |  Moduretic = amiloride + HCTZ  |  Dyazide / Maxzide = triamterene + HCTZ

ACE Inhibitors (ACEIs)

💊

lisinopril / enalapril / captopril — ACE Inhibitors (end in "-pril")

Mechanism of Action

Inhibits angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE), blocking the conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II. To understand why this matters, follow the full RAAS pathway: when BP falls, the kidneys secrete renin → renin converts angiotensinogen (an inactive protein from the liver) into angiotensin I → ACE (produced in the lungs) converts angiotensin I into angiotensin II, one of the most potent vasoconstrictors in the body.

Angiotensin II causes three harmful effects in HF patients: (1) vasoconstriction of arterioles and veins, which increases afterload; (2) SNS stimulation, which increases cardiac workload; and (3) aldosterone-mediated water retention, which increases preload. In heart failure, every one of these effects worsens the underlying failure — this is why ACEIs are the first medication used in HF.

By blocking ACE, the drug causes vasodilation ↓ AFTERLOAD, reduces aldosterone-driven Na⁺/H₂O retention ↓ PRELOAD, and increases bradykinin levels (which promotes further vasodilation but also causes the cough and angioedema risk).

Indications

HTN, HF, acute MI, prevention of MI and CVA.

Adverse Effects — The "ACEI Triad" Plus

First-dose hypotension is common — teach patients to rise slowly from sitting or lying positions. A persistent dry cough develops in many patients due to increased bradykinin, and this is the most common reason patients are switched to an ARB instead. Angioedema is a medical emergency involving swelling of the tongue, glottis, pharynx, lips, or eyes — also caused by increased bradykinin — and requires immediate treatment with epinephrine. Facial swelling is an early warning sign and a clinical red flag. Hyperkalemia can occur because less aldosterone means less K⁺ excretion — patients should limit K⁺ supplements, salt substitutes, and K⁺-sparing diuretics. ACEIs can also cause renal failure in patients with renal artery stenosis.

Nursing Considerations

Obtain BP before administering — hold if SBP < 90. Monitor BP carefully after the first dose, and also monitor K⁺, renal function (BUN/Cr), liver function, and WBC count. Teach the patient all adverse effects listed above and when to seek emergency care (any swelling of face, lips, or tongue). Change positions slowly, especially in elderly patients, to prevent orthostatic hypotension.

Expected Outcome

Lower BP; reduced volume in HF; myocardial remodeling protection post-MI; prevention of MI and CVA.

Angiotensin II Receptor Blockers (ARBs)

💊

losartan (Cozaar) / valsartan (Diovan) / irbesartan (Avapro) — ARBs (end in "-sartan")

Mechanism of Action

Blocks angiotensin II receptors directly in the tissues, preventing angiotensin II from binding and causing its usual effects. This results in vasodilation ↓ AFTERLOAD and decreased aldosterone release, which reduces Na⁺ and water retention ↓ PRELOAD.

Key Difference from ACEIs

ARBs do NOT increase bradykinin — which means no cough and lower angioedema risk (though angioedema can still rarely occur). This makes ARBs the go-to alternative for patients who cannot tolerate ACEIs due to cough. However, because ARBs do not raise bradykinin, their cardioprotective remodeling effects in HF are somewhat less favorable than ACEIs.

Adverse Effects

First-dose hypotension, angioedema (rare), hyperkalemia, renal failure with renal artery stenosis, headache, dizziness, and GI complaints.

Nursing Considerations

Do NOT use with an ACEI — the mechanisms overlap, increasing adverse effects without added benefit. Same K⁺ and renal monitoring as ACEIs.

Expected Outcome

Lower BP; reduced volume in HF; MI and CVA prevention.

Beta-Adrenergic Blockers

💊

metoprolol / atenolol / propranolol / labetalol — Beta Blockers (end in "-lol")

Mechanism of Action

Selectively inhibits beta-1 adrenergic receptors — the sympathetic "fight or flight" receptors primarily located in the heart. Normally, SNS stimulation causes increased HR, increased BP, and increased contractility — all of which increase cardiac workload. By blocking these beta-1 receptors, the drug produces the opposite effects: ↓ HR ↓ CONTRACTILITY → decreased CO and decreased O₂ demand.

Beta blockers also block beta-1 on juxtaglomerular cells in the kidney, reducing renin release and decreasing RAAS activation. With prolonged use, peripheral vascular resistance drops ↓ AFTERLOAD. In HF, excessive SNS activation is a compensatory mechanism that actually worsens failure over time — this is why beta blockers are commonly used alongside ACEIs in HF management, despite seeming counterintuitive.

Indications

First-line for exertional angina; HTN; acute MI; CAD; ventricular dysrhythmias; HF (specific agents only — carvedilol, bisoprolol, metoprolol XL).

Adverse Effects

Bradycardia is the primary concern. Beta blockers also mask hypoglycemia symptoms in diabetic patients by blocking the SNS response — the patient won't experience the usual shaking and tachycardia that normally warn of low blood glucose. Other effects include fatigue, weakness, hypotension, and bronchoconstriction (especially with non-selective agents like propranolol).

Nursing Considerations

Obtain BP and HR before administering — hold if SBP < 90 or HR < 60. Contraindicated in worsening HF, bradycardia, and heart block. Use with caution in diabetes and liver disease. Caution with asthma, especially with higher doses of metoprolol or non-selective agents like propranolol. Never discontinue abruptly — always taper to avoid rebound tachycardia and HTN. Change positions slowly in elderly patients. Teach the patient to monitor their own pulse daily.

Expected Outcome

Lower BP and HR; angina control.

Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs)

💊

amlodipine (Norvasc) / diltiazem (Cardizem) / verapamil (Calan) / nifedipine (Procardia)

Mechanism of Action

Inhibits or "blocks" the influx of extracellular calcium ions into the membranes of cardiac and vascular smooth muscle cells. Since contractility depends on intracellular Ca²⁺, blocking its entry produces three key effects: (1) dilation of coronary and systemic arteries, which lowers BP and improves coronary O₂ supply ↓ AFTERLOAD; (2) decreased myocardial contractility, which reduces cardiac workload and O₂ demand; and (3) slowed SA and AV node conduction ↓ HR.

Diltiazem is most commonly used specifically to slow HR and AV node conduction in atrial fibrillation and SVT. Because CCBs decrease contractility, they must be used with caution in HF — they can worsen an already-failing pump.

Indications

Angina (especially coronary vasospasm); HTN (second-line); controlling rapid HR in SVT or atrial fibrillation (except nifedipine, which is primarily a vasodilator).

Adverse Effects

Constipation is very common (decreased Ca²⁺ in GI smooth muscle slows motility). Other effects include bradycardia, hypotension (which creates a fall risk), and peripheral edema secondary to vasodilation.

Critical Drug Interactions

Digoxin: CCBs suppress the AV node and increase plasma digoxin levels — together this creates a high risk of severe bradycardia and digoxin toxicity. Beta blockers: Additive bradycardia and decreased contractility when combined. Grapefruit juice: Inhibits CYP3A4 enzymes, raising CCB drug levels — patients must be taught to avoid grapefruit.

Nursing Considerations

Obtain BP and HR before administering — hold if SBP < 90 or HR < 60. Increase dietary fiber and fluids to manage constipation. Measure I&O closely and assess fluid status due to potential for peripheral edema. Monitor liver and kidney function (metabolized in liver, excreted by kidneys). Change positions slowly, especially in elderly patients.

Expected Outcome

Lower BP and HR; angina control.

♥ High-Yield Takeaways — Hypertension Pharmacology
  • Diuretics ↓ preload (volume); ACEIs/ARBs ↓ afterload + preload (RAAS block); beta blockers ↓ HR + contractility + renin; CCBs ↓ afterload + HR.
  • Loop vs. thiazide calcium distinction: Loops Lose calcium; Thiazides hoard it.
  • K⁺-sparing diuretics + ACEIs/ARBs = hyperkalemia risk — do not combine without careful monitoring.
  • ACEI cough → switch to ARB. Angioedema with either → emergency; treat with epinephrine.
  • Digoxin interacts dangerously with loop diuretics (hypokalemia → toxicity) AND CCBs (AV suppression + elevated levels).

Heart FailureFrom Compensated Pump Failure to Cardiogenic Shock

Definition & Etiology

Heart failure (HF) is a clinical syndrome in which the heart is unable to pump sufficient blood to meet the metabolic demands of the body. It represents decreased cardiac output due to impaired myocardial function. HF can be acute (post-MI) or chronic (progressive). The most severe form of acute HF is cardiogenic shock.

Common causes of chronic HF: hypertension, coronary artery disease, and valvular disease.

Left Ventricular Heart Failure

Left-sided HF produces poor systemic perfusion (↓ CO) and pulmonary congestion (blood backs up into pulmonary vasculature). It has two subtypes:

Systolic HF (HFrEF)

HF with Reduced Ejection Fraction

The problem is weak contraction — the ventricle cannot squeeze forcefully enough during systole. The body compensates by increasing preload (more volume) and afterload (higher PVR/SBP), but ejection remains poor. EF drops below 40% (normal 50–70%), meaning less than half the blood in the ventricle is being pumped out. Blood accumulates in pulmonary vessels, decreasing effective CO. Patients with HFrEF are at high risk for sudden cardiac death.

Diastolic HF (HFpEF)

HF with Preserved Ejection Fraction

The problem is stiff filling — the LV cannot relax adequately during diastole, so the stiff ventricle prevents proper filling. Even though the heart squeezes normally (EF is preserved), CO drops because less blood enters the chamber in the first place. More pressure is needed to move the same volume. This type is especially common in older adults, women, patients with chronic HTN, and those with undetected CAD. Signs and symptoms are identical to systolic HF.

Signs & Symptoms — Left-Sided HF (Pulmonary Congestion)

🫁

Assessment Findings — Think "Lungs"

Because the left ventricle cannot pump blood forward effectively, blood backs up into the pulmonary vasculature. You will hear crackles (rales) in the lung bases bilaterally and possibly wheezes. The patient will be tachycardic and tachypneic as the body compensates. As pulmonary edema worsens, you may see frothy, pink-tinged sputum — this is a late, ominous sign that fluid is flooding the alveoli. On auscultation, listen for an S3 heart sound (the pathological "gallop"), which indicates ventricular volume overload. Patients often report orthopnea (difficulty breathing when lying flat — they need extra pillows) and paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (PND), where they wake up suddenly at night gasping for air.

Right Ventricular Heart Failure

When the right ventricle fails, it cannot empty completely. Blood backs up into the venous system, causing systemic congestion. Causes include left ventricular failure (most common), right ventricular MI, or pulmonary hypertension.

🩸

Assessment Findings — Think "Systemic / Venous Congestion"

When the right ventricle fails, blood backs up into the systemic venous circulation rather than the lungs. Look for jugular venous distention (JVD) — a visible sign that venous pressure is elevated. The liver and spleen become engorged (hepatomegaly and splenomegaly), which can cause anorexia and nausea. Fluid leaks into tissues, producing dependent edema in the legs and sacrum, swollen hands and fingers, and ascites (a distended, fluid-filled abdomen). Weight gain is one of the most reliable indicators of fluid retention — the patient should be weighed daily. Patients may also notice polyuria at night (nocturia) as fluid redistributes when they lie down. Blood pressure may be elevated from excess volume or decreased if the heart is too weak to pump effectively.

💡

Exam Pearl — Left vs. Right HF

Left = Lungs (pulmonary congestion: crackles, dyspnea, pink frothy sputum, orthopnea, PND, S3).
Right = Rest of the body (systemic venous congestion: JVD, hepatomegaly, peripheral edema, ascites, weight gain).
Left-sided failure is the most common cause of right-sided failure.

Cardiogenic Shock

🚨

Report Immediately — Life-Threatening Emergency

Cardiogenic shock is the most severe form of acute HF. Early detection is essential because undiagnosed cardiogenic shock has a very high mortality rate.

The classic presentation includes tachycardia with hypotension (SBP < 90 mmHg, or more than 30 mmHg below the patient's baseline). Renal perfusion drops, causing oliguria (UOP < 0.5–1 mL/kg/hr). The skin becomes cold and clammy with poor peripheral pulses as blood is shunted to vital organs. Cerebral perfusion decreases, leading to agitation, restlessness, or confusion. The patient also develops pulmonary congestion with tachypnea and may have continuing chest discomfort. If you see this cluster of findings in a post-MI patient, notify the provider immediately.

HF Compensatory Mechanisms — Why They Eventually Fail

🔬

Compensatory Cascade → Decompensation

1. Sympathetic nervous system (SNS) stimulation. The body detects falling CO and responds by increasing HR and vasoconstriction, temporarily boosting cardiac output. However, sustained SNS activation increases myocardial O₂ demand, and over time, CO actually declines because the heart is working harder but not more effectively.

2. RAAS activation. The kidneys sense decreased perfusion and trigger the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, retaining sodium and water to increase preload and afterload. This initially supports CO, but over time the resulting volume overload and increased resistance worsen heart failure further.

3. Natriuretic peptides (BNP). Stretched ventricles release BNP as a counter-regulatory attempt to promote natriuresis (sodium excretion by the kidneys). BNP level is a key diagnostic lab for heart failure — elevated BNP correlates with ventricular stretch and HF severity.

4. ADH (vasopressin) release. This causes additional water retention, compounding the volume overload that is already worsening the failing heart.

All four of these mechanisms are initially compensatory but ultimately pathological — and they are the exact targets of HF pharmacologic therapy. ACEIs block RAAS, beta blockers block SNS over-activation, diuretics remove the excess volume, and inotropes address the contractility deficit.

Pharmacology — Heart Failure

Diuretics (furosemide, HCTZ, spironolactone), ACEIs (enalapril), and ARBs (losartan) are used in HF with the same mechanisms and considerations as described in the HTN section above. Refer to Section 3 for full drug details. The following entries cover HF-specific agents.

Beta Blockers for Chronic HF

💊

carvedilol (Coreg) / bisoprolol / metoprolol XL (Toprol XL)

Mechanism of Action (in HF)

Protects the heart from excessive sympathetic stimulation and dysrhythmias. In HF, the SNS is chronically over-activated as a compensatory mechanism — but over time this excess stimulation worsens the failing heart. Beta blockers counteract this by blocking beta-1 receptors, which improves LV function, increases exercise tolerance, and slows HF progression.

Adverse Effects

Fluid retention and worsening of HF may occur paradoxically during the first weeks of therapy (the heart initially relies on that sympathetic support). Other side effects include fatigue, hypotension, and bradycardia or heart block.

Nursing Considerations

The patient must be euvolemic (volume-stable) and hemodynamically stable before starting a beta blocker for HF — do not initiate during acute decompensation. Start at the lowest dose and titrate slowly ("start low, go slow"). Monitor closely for worsening HF symptoms, especially early in therapy.

Expected Outcome

Improved LVEF, increased exercise tolerance, slowed HF progression, reduced hospitalization, and most importantly — prolonged survival.

Cardiac Glycoside

💊

digoxin (Lanoxin)

Mechanism of Action

A positive inotrope that works by inhibiting the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump on cardiac cells. This increases intracellular Na⁺, which in turn increases intracellular Ca²⁺, resulting in a stronger contraction ↑ CONTRACTILITY. Digoxin also slows HR through vagal stimulation ↓ HR, which decreases cardiac workload.

Indications

Chronic HF (second-line treatment when symptoms persist despite first-line therapy); atrial fibrillation (rate control).

Adverse Effects

Bradycardia is the most common cardiac effect. The most dangerous adverse effect is digoxin toxicity, which is dramatically potentiated by hypokalemia. Signs of toxicity include bradycardia, nausea and vomiting, visual disturbances (yellow-green halos, blurred vision), anorexia, fatigue, and potentially lethal dysrhythmias.

Nursing Considerations

Check apical pulse for a full 60 seconds before every dose — hold if HR < 60 bpm and notify the provider. Monitor serum digoxin levels regularly (therapeutic range: 0.5–2.0 ng/mL). Monitor K⁺ closely, because hypokalemia allows digoxin to bind more aggressively to cardiac Na⁺/K⁺ pumps, triggering toxicity — even at "normal" digoxin levels. K⁺ monitoring becomes especially critical when the patient is also taking diuretics that deplete K⁺. Antidote: digoxin immune Fab (Digibind).

Expected Outcome

Increased contractility and improved cardiac output.

🚩

Critical Connection — The Digoxin-Diuretic-Potassium Triangle

Many HF patients take digoxin + a loop or thiazide diuretic simultaneously. The diuretic depletes K⁺; low K⁺ amplifies digoxin's effects on the heart → toxicity (bradycardia, dysrhythmias, visual changes, GI symptoms). Always monitor potassium when a patient is on digoxin and a K⁺-wasting diuretic. A K⁺-sparing diuretic (spironolactone) may be added specifically to prevent this complication.

Sympathomimetic Inotrope

💊

dobutamine

Mechanism of Action

A selective beta-1 receptor agonist that directly stimulates the heart to contract more forcefully ↑ CONTRACTILITY, improving cardiac performance and CO. Unlike digoxin (which works on ion pumps), dobutamine mimics sympathetic stimulation at the receptor level for rapid effect.

Indications

Short-term IV management of acute or decompensated HF (e.g., post-STEMI with HF requiring immediate CO support).

Adverse Effects

Tachycardia (the heart is being stimulated) and phlebitis at the IV site.

Nursing Considerations

Must be given IV only — this is a critical-care medication. Beta blockers will antagonize its effect (they block the same receptors dobutamine is trying to activate), while nitroprusside has a synergistic effect. Monitor BP, HR, UOP, and peripheral pulses continuously. Monitor labs: K⁺, BUN, creatinine.

Expected Outcome

Increased CO without excessive tachycardia; increased UOP; improved HF symptoms (decreased SOB, decreased orthopnea, enhanced exercise tolerance).

Vasodilator

💊

nitroglycerin (NTG) — Short & Long-Acting Nitrate Forms

Available Forms

Nitroglycerin sublingual / Nitrostat — short-acting. Isosorbide mononitrate / Imdur, isosorbide dinitrate / Isordil, Nitrodur transdermal patches — long-acting.

Mechanism of Action

Relaxes and dilates both coronary arterial vessels and systemic venous smooth muscle. The venodilation decreases the amount of blood returning to the heart ↓ PRELOAD — with less blood to pump, cardiac output demands drop and workload decreases. The arteriolar dilation reduces systemic resistance ↓ AFTERLOAD. Additionally, dilation of the coronary arteries improves blood flow to the heart and relieves vasospasm-related angina. In ACS, nitroglycerin can limit infarct size and improve LV function.

Indications

Angina (primary use); HF related to MI; HTN associated with ACS.

Adverse Effects

Headache is the most common side effect — nitrates dilate cerebral blood vessels, causing what is essentially a mini-migraine. This is a sign the drug is pharmacologically active, not an allergic reaction. Other effects include orthostatic hypotension and reflex tachycardia.

Nursing Considerations

For acute chest pain, give sublingual 0.4 mg every 5 minutes for a maximum of 3 doses. If pain persists, determine whether IV NTG should be initiated. For IV administration, use a vented glass bottle with the manufacturer's tubing. Withhold if hypotensive, HR < 50, or HR > 100.

Withhold if the patient has taken any erectile dysfunction medication (sildenafil/Viagra, tadalafil/Cialis) within 24 hours — these are also nitrate-type vasodilators, and the synergistic effect can cause dramatic, life-threatening hypotension.

Tolerance is a common and serious problem with long-acting nitrates. The drug loses effectiveness if transdermal patches remain on continuously. Teach the patient to remove patches at bedtime and reapply in the morning, maintaining a 10–12 hour nitrate-free interval. At home, teach: take SL × 1 dose for chest pain, and if not relieved, call 911. Store SL tablets in the original dark glass container at room temperature and note the expiration date.

Expected Outcome

Angina relieved; cardiac muscle damage prevented or limited.

Opioid for Acute HF

💊

morphine sulfate

Mechanism of Action

Binds to opioid receptors, producing generalized CNS depression that decreases O₂ demand by lowering HR and BP ↓ AFTERLOAD. Morphine also causes venodilation, which reduces the amount of blood returning to the heart ↓ PRELOAD. The anxiolytic effect reduces sympathetic drive, further decreasing cardiac workload.

Indications

Dyspnea relief in acute HF; chest pain management in acute MI.

Adverse Effects

Hypotension, bradycardia, constipation, sedation, and most critically — respiratory depression. This is the primary safety concern with opioid administration.

Nursing Considerations

Monitor respirations closely — withhold if respiratory rate is below 12 breaths per minute. Also monitor BP and HR. Change positions slowly to prevent orthostatic hypotension. Antidote: naloxone (Narcan).

Expected Outcome

Dyspnea relief; reduced anxiety and cardiac workload.

Drugs That Worsen Heart Failure

⚠️

Avoid in HF Patients

NSAIDs (ibuprofen, diclofenac) promote sodium retention and peripheral vasoconstriction, which directly worsens heart failure. They also intensify the toxicity of diuretics and ACEIs and increase the risk of thrombosis. NSAIDs are contraindicated in HF, ischemic heart disease, PVD, and cerebrovascular disease.

Calcium channel blockers decrease contractility, which can worsen pump failure in most HF patients. Because contractility depends on intracellular Ca²⁺, blocking its entry reduces the already-weakened heart's ability to contract effectively.

Most antidysrhythmic agents have negative inotropic effects that further depress cardiac function.

Clinical Reasoning PatternsThink Like a Nurse — Prioritization, Red Flags, Decision-Making

Assessment vs. Intervention — "What Should the Nurse Do First?"

🩺

Decision Framework

Rule 1 — Assess before you act (unless the patient is in immediate danger). If the question asks "what should the nurse do first?" and one option is an assessment and another is an intervention, choose the assessment — unless the patient is coding, in shock, or having an anaphylactic reaction.

Rule 2 — ABC prioritization. Airway → Breathing → Circulation. A patient with pulmonary edema (frothy pink sputum, crackles, dyspnea) is a breathing priority. A patient in cardiogenic shock (hypotension, tachycardia, oliguria) is a circulation priority.

Rule 3 — Unstable vital signs trump everything. If BP < 90 systolic, HR < 50 or > 150, RR < 12, or O₂ sat < 90%, intervene immediately and notify the provider.

Preload vs. Afterload — Linking Drugs to Hemodynamics

💡

The Exam Loves This Connection

↓ Preload drugs (reduce venous return / blood volume): diuretics, nitroglycerin (venodilation), morphine, ACEIs, ARBs.

↓ Afterload drugs (reduce systemic vascular resistance): ACEIs, ARBs, CCBs, beta blockers (long-term), nitroglycerin (arteriolar dilation), morphine.

↑ Contractility drugs (positive inotropes): digoxin, dobutamine.

When a question asks "which drug decreases preload?" — think volume reduction and venodilation. When it asks "which drug decreases afterload?" — think arterial vasodilation. When it asks "which drug increases contractility?" — think inotropes.

Case 1 — The Post-MI Patient with New Heart Failure
SCENARIO

Mr. R., 62, was admitted 48 hours ago for a STEMI. He is now complaining of increasing SOB, especially when lying flat. Assessment reveals: HR 108, BP 98/68, RR 28, O₂ sat 90% on room air, bilateral crackles in lung bases, S3 heart sound, frothy sputum. Current medications: atorvastatin, aspirin, heparin drip, metoprolol.

QUESTIONS

1. What type of heart failure is Mr. R. developing, and what assessment findings support this?

2. What is the nurse's priority action?

3. What medications would you anticipate the provider to order?

4. Which current medication should the nurse question and why?

1. Left-sided (ventricular) heart failure — specifically acute systolic HF (HFrEF) post-MI. Supporting findings: bilateral crackles (pulmonary congestion), S3 gallop (volume overload), orthopnea (SOB when flat), frothy sputum (pulmonary edema), tachycardia and tachypnea (compensatory), hypotension (↓ CO).

2. Priority: Position upright (high Fowler's) to reduce preload and ease breathing; apply supplemental O₂; notify provider immediately. With SBP of 98, this patient is approaching cardiogenic shock territory. Continuous monitoring is essential.

3. Anticipated orders: IV furosemide (acute diuresis to ↓ pulmonary congestion); IV nitroglycerin (↓ preload/afterload if BP tolerates); morphine (dyspnea relief, ↓ preload); dobutamine (if CO continues to decline — ↑ contractility). IV enalapril may follow for afterload reduction and remodeling.

4. Question metoprolol. Beta blockers should only be initiated in HF when the patient is euvolemic and hemodynamically stable. Mr. R. is hypotensive (SBP 98), tachycardic (compensatory), and in acute decompensation. Metoprolol could further ↓ HR, ↓ contractility, and ↓ BP, worsening his condition. Hold and notify the provider.

Case 2 — The Hypertensive Patient with Medication Complications
SCENARIO

Mrs. T., 74, has a history of HTN and chronic HF. Medications: furosemide 40 mg daily, digoxin 0.125 mg daily, lisinopril 10 mg daily, KCl supplement. Today's labs: K⁺ 3.1 mEq/L, Na⁺ 132 mEq/L, BUN 32, Cr 1.6, digoxin level 2.4 ng/mL. She reports nausea, seeing "halos around lights," and feeling "not right." HR: 48 bpm.

QUESTIONS

1. What is the primary problem, and what caused it?

2. What medications should the nurse hold?

3. What is the priority intervention?

4. What teaching would you provide upon discharge to prevent recurrence?

1. Digoxin toxicity, triggered by hypokalemia from furosemide. The classic triad is present: bradycardia (HR 48), GI symptoms (nausea), and visual disturbances (halos). Digoxin level is 2.4 ng/mL (therapeutic 0.5–2.0). The mechanism: furosemide depleted K⁺ despite KCl supplement → low K⁺ allows digoxin to bind more aggressively to cardiac Na⁺/K⁺ pumps → toxicity.

2. Hold digoxin (supratherapeutic level + toxic symptoms). Hold furosemide (contributing to hypokalemia). Hold lisinopril if BP is low. Continue KCl or anticipate increased K⁺ replacement.

3. Notify provider STAT. Place on continuous cardiac monitoring. Anticipate: IV potassium replacement (carefully — monitor cardiac rhythm); digoxin immune Fab if dysrhythmias develop or toxicity is severe. Monitor I&O.

4. Teach: Take KCl as prescribed and eat K⁺-rich foods (bananas, oranges, potatoes). Check pulse daily before taking digoxin — hold and call provider if HR < 60. Report nausea, visual changes, or "feeling off." Keep all lab appointments (K⁺, digoxin levels, renal function). Weigh daily; report > 2 lb gain in 24 hours.

QUESTION 1

A patient taking furosemide 40 mg daily for HF and HTN presents for a routine visit. Which electrolyte imbalances should the nurse anticipate?

A) Low K⁺, Na⁺, Mg²⁺; elevated Ca²⁺
B) Low K⁺, Na⁺; elevated Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺
C) Low K⁺, Na⁺, Mg²⁺, and Ca²⁺

B) Administer the morning dose of digoxin

Answer: C. K⁺ is critically low (normal 3.5–5.0) and the patient is bradycardic (HR 54 < 60 = hold digoxin threshold). Hold both drugs: digoxin (for low HR and toxicity risk) and furosemide (contributing to K⁺ loss). Notify the provider for K⁺ replacement orders and digoxin level check. Eating a banana (D) addresses long-term K⁺ but is not the priority acute intervention.

(D) K⁺ 3.0 is hypokalemia and increases dig toxicity risk.

Question 3 — Therapeutic Response

A client with hypertension is prescribed lisinopril 10 mg daily. Which evaluation would indicate a therapeutic response to the medication?

A) The client develops a persistent dry cough
B) Blood pressure reading of 124/76 mmHg
C) Serum potassium of 5.8 mEq/L
D) The client reports swelling of the lips and tongue

Answer: B. The therapeutic goal of lisinopril (ACEI) for HTN is BP below 130/80 mmHg. A reading of 124/76 meets this target. (A) Dry cough is a common side effect from increased bradykinin — it's expected but not a therapeutic response. (C) K⁺ 5.8 is dangerously elevated — hyperkalemia is an adverse effect of ACEIs. (D) Lip/tongue swelling is angioedema, a life-threatening emergency requiring immediate ACEI discontinuation and epinephrine.

Question 4 — Determining Effectiveness

A client with right-sided heart failure is prescribed spironolactone (Aldactone) 25 mg daily. What assessment would be best to determine the effectiveness of this medication?

A) Auscultate lung sounds for crackles bilaterally
B) Monitor daily weights and assess for decreased peripheral edema
C) Check for the presence of an S3 heart sound
D) Obtain a serum BNP level

Answer: B. Right-sided HF causes systemic venous congestion — peripheral edema, ascites, weight gain. Spironolactone is a K⁺-sparing diuretic that reduces fluid volume (decreases preload). The best way to assess diuretic effectiveness in right-sided HF is daily weight and peripheral edema assessment. Weight is the most reliable indicator of fluid status change. (A) Crackles are a left-sided HF finding. (C) S3 reflects volume overload but is not the most practical ongoing assessment. (D) BNP is useful diagnostically but not the best day-to-day effectiveness measure for a single diuretic.

Question 5 — Determining Effectiveness

A client with atherosclerosis and hyperlipidemia is prescribed atorvastatin (Lipitor) 40 mg daily. What assessment would be best to determine the effectiveness of this medication?

A) Monitor the client's blood pressure weekly
B) Assess for muscle pain and dark urine
C) Review fasting lipid panel results for decreased LDL-C levels
D) Monitor the client's heart rate and rhythm

Answer: C. Atorvastatin inhibits HMG-CoA reductase to decrease LDL-C production. The best way to determine effectiveness is to check whether lipid levels have improved — specifically decreased LDL-C on a fasting lipid panel. (A) Statins do not directly lower BP. (B) Muscle pain and dark urine are signs of rhabdomyolysis — this assesses for adverse effects, not effectiveness. (D) HR and rhythm are not directly affected by statins.

Question 6 — Determining Effectiveness

A client with stable angina is prescribed sublingual nitroglycerin PRN. What assessment would be best to determine the effectiveness of this medication?

A) Blood pressure decreases by 20 mmHg after administration
B) The client reports headache onset within 2 minutes
C) The client states chest pain has resolved after taking the medication
D) Heart rate increases from 72 to 96 bpm

Answer: C. Nitroglycerin dilates coronary arteries (improving O₂ supply) and reduces preload via venodilation (decreasing O₂ demand). The purpose of SL NTG is to relieve angina — so pain relief is the therapeutic response. (A) BP drop is an expected side effect, not the measure of effectiveness. (B) Headache is the most common side effect (cerebral vasodilation) — it tells you the drug is pharmacologically active, but it is not the therapeutic goal. (D) Reflex tachycardia is a side effect, not effectiveness.

Question 7 — Pre-Administration Assessment

A client with chronic heart failure is prescribed metoprolol succinate (Toprol XL) 25 mg daily. What assessment is needed prior to administering this medication?

A) Serum potassium level
B) Heart rate and blood pressure
C) Lung sounds for wheezing
D) Serum digoxin level

Answer: B. Metoprolol is a beta blocker that decreases HR and BP by blocking sympathetic beta-1 receptors. You must check HR and BP before every dose — hold if HR < 60 or SBP < 90 and notify the provider. (A) K⁺ is critical for digoxin and diuretics, not beta blockers primarily. (C) Lung sounds for wheezing are important to assess (beta blockers can cause bronchoconstriction), but HR and BP are the priority hold parameters. (D) Digoxin level is unrelated to metoprolol administration.

Question 8 — Pre-Administration Assessment

A client with heart failure is prescribed digoxin 0.125 mg daily. What assessment is needed prior to administering this medication?

A) Blood pressure and respiratory rate
B) Apical heart rate for a full 60 seconds
C) Bowel sounds in all four quadrants
D) Peripheral pulse oximetry reading

Answer: B. Digoxin slows heart rate and increases contractility. The critical pre-administration assessment is an apical pulse for a full 60 seconds — hold the medication and notify the provider if HR < 60 bpm. Apical (not radial) is used because it is the most accurate for detecting irregular rhythms and bradycardia. (A) BP and RR are important general assessments but are not the specific hold parameter for digoxin. (C) Bowel sounds are unrelated. (D) Pulse oximetry does not guide digoxin administration.

Question 9 — Pre-Administration Assessment

A client with acute coronary syndrome is prescribed sublingual nitroglycerin 0.4 mg. What assessment is needed prior to administering this medication?

A) Serum troponin level
B) Blood pressure and whether the client has taken erectile dysfunction medication in the past 24 hours
C) Serum potassium and magnesium levels
D) Apical heart rate for 60 seconds

Answer: B. Nitroglycerin causes vasodilation that lowers BP — withhold if SBP < 90 or HR < 50 or > 100. Additionally, nitroglycerin is absolutely contraindicated if the patient has taken any erectile dysfunction medication (sildenafil, tadalafil) within 24 hours, as the synergistic vasodilation can cause life-threatening hypotension. This is a critical safety check that must happen before every dose. (A) Troponin confirms MI but does not determine whether NTG can be given safely. (C) Electrolytes are more relevant to diuretics and digoxin. (D) While HR is considered, BP and ED-medication history are the priority NTG assessments.

Question 10 — Lab Follow-Up

The nurse administered heparin IV to a client with deep vein thrombosis. Four hours later, the lab results return: aPTT 108 seconds, platelets 142,000/mm³, Hgb 10.2 g/dL. What lab values require follow-up or intervention?

A) The platelet count of 142,000/mm³
B) The aPTT of 108 seconds
C) The hemoglobin of 10.2 g/dL
D) All of the above require follow-up

Answer: B. The therapeutic aPTT for heparin is 60–80 seconds (1.5–2× normal of ~40 seconds). An aPTT of 108 seconds is supratherapeutic — the patient is at significant risk for hemorrhage. The nurse should hold the heparin infusion and notify the provider immediately. Have protamine sulfate (heparin antidote) available. (A) Platelets of 142,000 are within normal range (150,000–400,000 is normal, but mild decreases can occur; concern for HIT is typically <100,000 or >50% drop). (C) Hgb of 10.2 is mildly low but not acutely critical in this context. The aPTT is the most urgent finding.

Question 11 — Lab Follow-Up

The nurse administered furosemide 40 mg IV to a client with acute decompensated heart failure. The following labs return: K⁺ 2.8 mEq/L, Na⁺ 131 mEq/L, BUN 34, Cr 1.8, digoxin level 1.8 ng/mL. The client also takes digoxin. Which lab value requires the most urgent follow-up?

A) Na⁺ of 131 mEq/L
B) Digoxin level of 1.8 ng/mL
C) K⁺ of 2.8 mEq/L
D) BUN of 34

Answer: C. K⁺ 2.8 is critically low (normal 3.5–5.0). In a patient also taking digoxin, hypokalemia is the most dangerous finding because it dramatically increases the risk of digoxin toxicity — even though the digoxin level itself (1.8 ng/mL) is technically within therapeutic range. Low K⁺ allows digoxin to bind more aggressively to cardiac Na⁺/K⁺ pumps, causing toxicity at "normal" levels. The nurse should hold both furosemide and digoxin, notify the provider, and anticipate IV potassium replacement with cardiac monitoring. (A) Na⁺ 131 is mildly low but less immediately dangerous. (B) Dig level 1.8 is technically therapeutic, but unsafe with this K⁺ level. (D) BUN 34 reflects reduced renal perfusion — expected in HF but not the most urgent finding here.

Question 12 — Lab Follow-Up

The nurse administered enalapril 5 mg IV to a client with acute heart failure. The following labs return: K⁺ 5.6 mEq/L, BUN 38, Cr 2.1, Na⁺ 136 mEq/L. Which lab value requires follow-up?

A) Na⁺ of 136 mEq/L
B) BUN of 38
C) Cr of 2.1
D) K⁺ of 5.6 mEq/L

Answer: D. ACE inhibitors decrease aldosterone, which means the kidneys retain potassium. K⁺ 5.6 is hyperkalemia (normal 3.5–5.0) and can cause lethal cardiac dysrhythmias. The nurse should hold enalapril, notify the provider, place the patient on cardiac monitoring, and anticipate treatment. Also note: BUN 38 and Cr 2.1 are elevated and suggest renal impairment — ACEIs can worsen renal function, especially in patients with renal artery stenosis. These values also warrant follow-up. (A) Na⁺ 136 is normal (135–145). However, the K⁺ is the most dangerous and requires the most urgent intervention.

Question 13 — Discharge Teaching

A client with heart failure is prescribed carvedilol (Coreg) 6.25 mg twice daily. What teaching is needed prior to being discharged on this medication?

A) "Take your pulse each morning. If it is below 60, hold the medication and call your doctor."
B) "You can stop this medication once you feel better."
C) "Increase your potassium intake with bananas and orange juice."
D) "Take this medication on an empty stomach for best absorption."

Answer: A. Carvedilol is a beta blocker that decreases HR and BP. Patients must be taught to check their pulse daily and hold the medication if HR drops below 60, then notify the provider. They should also rise slowly (orthostatic hypotension risk) and never stop the medication abruptly (rebound tachycardia and HTN). (B) HF medications are lifelong — stopping when "feeling better" can cause acute decompensation. (C) K⁺ supplementation is for patients on K⁺-wasting diuretics, not beta blockers. (D) Carvedilol should actually be taken with food to slow absorption and reduce orthostatic effects.

Question 14 — Discharge Teaching

A client with coronary artery disease is prescribed nitroglycerin sublingual tablets PRN and isosorbide mononitrate (Imdur) daily. What teaching is needed prior to being discharged on these medications?

A) "Place the SL tablet under your tongue, and if pain persists after one dose, call 911."
B) "Store your NTG tablets in a clear plastic pill organizer for convenience."
C) "The Imdur patch should remain on 24 hours a day for continuous protection."
D) "If you experience a headache, this means you are allergic to the medication."

Answer: A. Current teaching for SL NTG at home: take one dose, and if chest pain is not relieved, call 911 immediately. (B) NTG must be stored in its original dark glass container at room temperature — light, heat, and moisture degrade the drug. A plastic pill organizer would reduce potency. (C) Imdur is an oral long-acting nitrate (not a patch), but the principle still applies: tolerance develops with continuous nitrate use. A nitrate-free interval (typically removing patches at bedtime) is essential for all long-acting forms. (D) Headache is the most common side effect of all nitrates — it occurs because the drug dilates cerebral blood vessels (a "mini migraine"). It is NOT an allergic reaction and is actually a sign the drug is pharmacologically active.

Question 15 — Discharge Teaching

A client with hypertension and edema is prescribed spironolactone (Aldactone) 25 mg daily. What teaching is needed prior to being discharged on this medication?

A) "You should increase your potassium intake by eating more bananas."
B) "Weigh yourself daily and report a gain of more than 2 pounds in 24 hours."
C) "This medication may cause you to cough. Let your doctor know if it bothers you."
D) "Avoid salt substitutes and potassium supplements while taking this medication."

Answer: D. Spironolactone is a K⁺-sparing diuretic that blocks aldosterone, retaining potassium. Patients must avoid additional potassium sources — including potassium-based salt substitutes (like "lite salt" or "No Salt," which contain KCl) and K⁺ supplements — to prevent dangerous hyperkalemia. (A) is the opposite of what's needed — increasing K⁺ intake with a K⁺-sparing diuretic risks hyperkalemia. (B) is good general HF teaching and applies to all diuretics, but the question asks what is needed specifically for spironolactone — the K⁺ restriction teaching is the priority unique to this drug. (C) Cough is associated with ACE inhibitors (from increased bradykinin), not spironolactone.

Question 16 — Pathophysiology & Drug Connection

A client with chronic left-sided systolic heart failure (EF 28%) is prescribed enalapril, carvedilol, furosemide, and digoxin. The student nurse asks: "Why does this patient need so many different medications?"

Which response by the nurse best explains the rationale for this combination?

A) "Each medication treats a different symptom of heart failure independently."
B) "The combination targets the compensatory mechanisms that initially help but ultimately worsen heart failure — RAAS over-activation, excessive sympathetic stimulation, volume overload, and decreased contractility."
C) "The provider prescribes multiple drugs to ensure at least one of them works."
D) "All of these drugs increase the heart's contractility through different pathways."

Answer: B. This question tests your understanding of HF pathophysiology and why each drug class is used. In HF, the body activates compensatory mechanisms that eventually become maladaptive: Enalapril (ACEI) blocks RAAS → ↓ afterload and ↓ preload by preventing angiotensin II–mediated vasoconstriction and aldosterone-driven fluid retention. Carvedilol (beta blocker) blocks excessive SNS stimulation → protects the heart from increased workload, reduces HR, and slows HF progression. Furosemide (loop diuretic) removes excess fluid → ↓ preload, relieving pulmonary congestion and edema. Digoxin (positive inotrope) increases contractility → improves the heart's ability to eject blood. Together, they target every component of the CO equation. (A) is oversimplified. (C) is incorrect reasoning. (D) is factually wrong — only digoxin increases contractility; the others work on different targets.

Question 17 — Preload & Afterload Application

A nursing student is reviewing medications for a client with acute decompensated heart failure. The student needs to identify which drugs will reduce the client's preload and which will reduce afterload.

Which grouping correctly categorizes these drugs?

A) Preload: furosemide, enalapril. Afterload: metoprolol, nitroglycerin.
B) Preload: furosemide, nitroglycerin, morphine. Afterload: enalapril, amlodipine, metoprolol.
C) Preload: digoxin, dobutamine. Afterload: furosemide, spironolactone.
D) Preload: amlodipine, metoprolol. Afterload: furosemide, morphine.

Answer: B. Preload reducers decrease blood volume returning to the heart: furosemide (removes fluid), nitroglycerin (venodilation → blood pools in veins → less returns to heart), and morphine (venodilation + decreased sympathetic drive). Afterload reducers decrease systemic vascular resistance so the LV can eject more easily: enalapril (blocks angiotensin II vasoconstriction), amlodipine (CCB → arterial vasodilation), and metoprolol (long-term ↓ PVR). Note: some drugs affect both — enalapril and NTG both reduce preload AND afterload. The question asks for the primary categorization. (C) is wrong because digoxin and dobutamine are inotropes (↑ contractility), not preload drugs. (D) reverses the categories.

Question 18 — Comprehensive Application

A client with hypertension is prescribed diltiazem (Cardizem) 120 mg daily. The client also takes digoxin for atrial fibrillation and reports drinking grapefruit juice every morning. The nurse reviews the medication list.

Which concern should the nurse prioritize?

A) Diltiazem may cause constipation requiring dietary fiber supplementation
B) Diltiazem and digoxin together increase the risk of severe bradycardia and AV block, and grapefruit juice raises diltiazem levels — both interactions need to be addressed
C) The client should switch from grapefruit juice to orange juice for more potassium
D) Diltiazem will increase the effectiveness of digoxin, which is beneficial

Answer: B. Two critical drug interactions exist here. First: diltiazem (CCB) suppresses AV node conduction AND increases plasma digoxin levels — combined with digoxin's own HR-lowering effect, this creates a high risk for severe bradycardia, AV block, and digoxin toxicity. Second: grapefruit juice inhibits CYP3A4 enzymes → raises diltiazem blood levels → amplifies all CCB effects. The nurse must notify the provider about the drug-drug interaction and teach the patient to avoid grapefruit. (A) Constipation is a real CCB side effect but is not the priority safety concern here. (C) switches juice type without addressing the actual problem. (D) is incorrect — the "increased effectiveness" is actually increased toxicity risk, which is harmful.

Master Review — High-Yield TakeawaysThe "If You Remember Nothing Else" Section

♥ Physiology Fundamentals
  • CO = SV × HR; BP = CO × PVR. These two equations explain every drug mechanism and every disease process in this unit.
  • Preload = volume/stretch; Afterload = resistance; Contractility = force; HR = rate. Know which drugs affect which.
  • RAAS exists to maintain BP and renal perfusion — but chronic over-activation causes HTN, volume overload, and HF progression.
  • Frank-Starling: stretching helps contractility until the heart is overstretched — this is the point of decompensation.
♥ Pathophysiology Connections
  • Atherosclerosis → plaque rupture → thrombosis → MI/stroke. It is the upstream cause of nearly all perfusion disorders.
  • HTN ↑ afterload chronically → LV hypertrophy → eventually HF. HTN also accelerates atherosclerosis.
  • Left HF = LUNGS (crackles, orthopnea, S3, pink frothy sputum). Right HF = BODY (JVD, edema, ascites, hepatomegaly).
  • Cardiogenic shock = the end of the HF spectrum — SBP < 90, tachycardia, oliguria, cold/clammy. Report immediately.
♥ Pharmacology Must-Knows
  • Digoxin-Diuretic-Potassium Triangle: Loop/thiazide depletes K⁺ → ↑ digoxin toxicity. Always check K⁺.
  • ACEI cough → switch to ARB. Both can cause angioedema (treat with epinephrine) and hyperkalemia.
  • Never combine: two loops; ACEI + ARB; K⁺-sparing diuretic + ACEI/ARB (without careful monitoring).
  • Nitroglycerin + sildenafil within 24h = severe hypotension. Always ask.
  • Antidotes: Heparin → protamine sulfate. Digoxin → digoxin immune Fab. Opioids → naloxone. Angioedema → epinephrine.
♥ Nursing Priority Patterns
  • Assess before you intervene (unless life-threatening). ABC always applies.
  • Daily weights are the most reliable indicator of fluid status in HF (more reliable than I&O).
  • Hold and notify: digoxin if HR < 60; beta blockers if HR low or AV block; any antihypertensive if SBP < 90.
  • Every anticoagulant and antiplatelet question comes back to one thing: monitor for and teach signs of bleeding.
  • HF patients: restrict Na⁺, weigh daily, report > 2 lb/day or 5 lb/week gain, avoid NSAIDs.
