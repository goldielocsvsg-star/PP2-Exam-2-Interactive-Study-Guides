🫀 Core Concepts — How the Body Works

Understand the pathophysiology first. When you know why things happen, the symptoms and interventions make perfect sense.

🩺
Perfusion & Oxygen Delivery
Perfusion is the body's ability to deliver oxygenated blood to every tissue and organ. Think of your heart as the engine and your blood vessels as the highway. When that highway is blocked or the engine is weak, cells don't get oxygen — and without oxygen, organs fail.

Nurses assess perfusion by checking mental status (confused = brain hypoxia), skin color/temperature, capillary refill (<3 sec is normal), pulses, urine output (>30 mL/hr = adequate kidney perfusion), and SpO₂.
🚨 Red FlagConfusion + cool clammy skin + weak pulses + decreased UO = perfusion crisis → immediate intervention
Priority Assess First
💓
Cardiac Output (CO = HR × SV)
Cardiac output is how much blood the heart pumps per minute. It depends on heart rate and stroke volume — and stroke volume depends on three things:

Preload: How full the ventricle is before it squeezes. Too much = fluid overload. Too little = not enough to pump.
Afterload: The resistance the heart must pump against. High BP = high afterload = heart works harder and eventually fails.
Contractility: How forcefully the heart squeezes. Digoxin increases this.

Ejection fraction (EF): % of blood ejected per beat. Normal ≥55%. Low EF = systolic heart failure.
🧠 Think This WayLow CO → reduced organ perfusion → all the failure symptoms you'll see on the exam
CO = HR × SV EF ≥55%
📈
Hypertension
BP = Cardiac Output × Systemic Vascular Resistance. When vessels stay constricted (high SVR), blood pressure rises chronically. The RAAS system makes it worse — angiotensin II causes vasoconstriction AND aldosterone release → sodium/water retention → even more pressure.

The insidious part: HTN is called the "silent killer" because it causes no symptoms until organ damage occurs. Over time, it damages the heart (LVH, HF), kidneys (CKD), eyes (retinopathy), and brain (stroke).
🚨 Hypertensive EmergencyBP >180/120 with end-organ damage signs = call provider immediately. Assess neuro, cardiac, renal status.
Silent Killer Organ Damage
💔
CAD & Myocardial Infarction
Coronary artery disease = atherosclerotic plaque buildup inside coronary arteries → narrowing → reduced O₂ to heart muscle → ischemia (chest pain/angina).

In an MI, that plaque ruptures. A clot forms at the rupture site → complete occlusion → prolonged ischemia → myocardial cell death (necrosis) → permanent loss of contractility.

Classic symptoms: chest pain radiating to jaw/left arm, diaphoresis, dyspnea, nausea. Women often present atypically: fatigue, nausea, jaw pain without classic chest pain.

Troponin rises within 3-6 hours of injury → diagnostic gold standard.
🚨 STEMI EmergencyMONA = Morphine, Oxygen, Nitroglycerin, Aspirin. Prioritize reperfusion (PCI/thrombolytics) within 90 min of door arrival.
Troponin ↑ 90 Min Window
🫀
Heart Failure — Left vs Right
Left-sided HF: The left ventricle can't pump blood forward efficiently → blood backs up into the pulmonary circulation → fluid leaks into lung tissue → pulmonary edema. Symptoms: dyspnea, orthopnea, crackles, pink frothy sputum.

Right-sided HF: Right ventricle fails → blood backs up into systemic venous system → JVD (jugular vein distention), peripheral edema (legs/ankles), ascites, hepatomegaly.

Most common cause of right-sided HF? Left-sided HF that has progressed.

Daily weight is the #1 most sensitive indicator of fluid status. 1 kg = ~1 liter of fluid.
💡 Left = Lungs, Right = Rest of body
Daily Weight JVD = Right HF Crackles = Left HF
🦵
Peripheral Arterial Disease (PAD)
PAD = atherosclerosis in peripheral arteries (usually legs) → reduced blood flow to extremities. The hallmark symptom is intermittent claudication — cramping leg pain that occurs with activity and is relieved by rest (because muscles can't get enough O₂ under demand).

Advanced PAD: rest pain, non-healing wounds, pale/cool skin, decreased or absent pulses. Assess with ankle-brachial index (ABI) — normal ≥0.9.

Positioning: Keep legs dependent (gravity aids perfusion). Do NOT elevate legs — this worsens arterial flow.
🚨 6 P's of Acute Limb IschemiaPain, Pallor, Pulselessness, Paresthesia, Paralysis, Poikilothermia (cold) → surgical emergency
ABI <0.9 Legs Dependent
🧬
Normal Clotting Process
Hemostasis = the body's process of stopping bleeding. It occurs in 3 phases:

1. Vascular Spasm: Injured vessel constricts immediately to limit blood loss.
2. Platelet Plug: Platelets adhere to injured vessel, activate, and aggregate to form a temporary plug.
3. Coagulation Cascade: A chain of clotting factor reactions produces fibrin, which reinforces the platelet plug into a stable clot.

Intrinsic pathway → monitored by aPTT (used for heparin monitoring)
Extrinsic pathway → monitored by PT/INR (used for warfarin monitoring)
aPTT = Heparin INR = Warfarin
🩸
DVT & Pulmonary Embolism
Virchow's Triad explains why clots form: Blood stasis + Endothelial injury + Hypercoagulability.

DVT: Clot in deep vein (usually leg) → calf pain, warmth, redness, swelling. Often asymptomatic! Do NOT rub or massage — can dislodge clot.

PE: DVT clot breaks off → travels to pulmonary artery → blocks blood flow through lungs → can't oxygenate → sudden onset dyspnea, chest pain, tachycardia, hemoptysis, hypoxia. Life-threatening emergency.

Risk factors: immobility, surgery, cancer, pregnancy, obesity, oral contraceptives.
🚨 PE AlertSudden dyspnea + chest pain + tachycardia = presumed PE until proven otherwise. O₂, call provider, prep for CT angiography.
No Massage DVT Virchow's Triad
💧
Thrombocytopenia
Thrombocytopenia = low platelet count → impaired platelet plug formation → bleeding risk.

Normal platelets: 150,000–400,000/mm³. Critical: <50,000 = spontaneous bleeding risk. <20,000 = severe, may need transfusion.

Causes: bone marrow suppression, chemotherapy, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT — paradoxically causes clotting too!), autoimmune destruction.

Signs: petechiae (tiny red dots), purpura, easy bruising, prolonged bleeding, bleeding gums, internal hemorrhage (hematuria, melena).
🛡️ Nursing: Bleeding PrecautionsSoft toothbrush, electric razor, avoid injections/IM meds, fall precautions, monitor for occult bleeding.
Petechiae <50k = Danger HIT Watch

🎯 NCLEX Thinking Framework

NCLEX doesn't test memorization — it tests clinical reasoning. Use this 4-question framework on every question.

🩸
1. Is perfusion decreased?
Cues: confusion, cool skin, weak pulse, low UO, hypotension, pallor, cyanosis → Intervene to restore circulation. Position supine, O₂, notify provider.
🫁
2. Is oxygen delivery impaired?
Cues: low SpO₂, dyspnea, restlessness, tachycardia, cyanosis → O₂ therapy, positioning (HOB 45°), monitor ABGs, assess for PE/HF.
🩺
3. Is patient clotting or bleeding?
Clotting signs: swollen warm extremity, dyspnea, chest pain. Bleeding signs: petechiae, hematuria, hematoma, hypotension → Anticoagulants vs. bleeding precautions — opposite actions!
💊
4. Is this a medication safety issue?
Always check: HR before digoxin/beta-blockers (hold if <60), BP before antihypertensives (hold if hypotensive), INR/aPTT before anticoagulants, K⁺ with diuretics.

💡 Memory Boosters & Exam Traps

Mnemonics, cause-effect chains, and the classic traps professors love to test.

🔑 Drug Class Mnemonics

ACE inhibitors end in "-pril":
lisinopril, captopril, enalapril

ARBs end in "-sartan":
losartan, valsartan

Beta blockers end in "-olol":
metoprolol, propranolol, atenolol

CCBs end in "-dipine" (or verapamil):
nifedipine, amlodipine

For WARFARIN think "W-INR"
Warfarin → INR → 2–3 = therapeutic

🔗 Cause → Effect Chains

HTN → Increased afterload → LV hypertrophy → HF
HF → Low CO → RAAS activation → Fluid retention → Worse HF
Immobility → Stasis → DVT → PE → Hypoxia
Plaque → Rupture → Thrombus → Occlusion → MI
Furosemide → Diuresis → K⁺ loss → Hypokalemia → Digoxin toxicity ↑
Heparin → HIT → Platelets ↓ → Paradoxical clotting

🪤 Exam Traps — Classic Gotchas

⚠️ DVT massage trap: Never massage a suspected DVT — emboli risk. Students think massage helps. Wrong!
⚠️ PAD leg position: Keep legs dependent, NOT elevated. Opposite of venous insufficiency!
⚠️ Nitroglycerin: Patient MUST be seated/lying before giving — causes sudden hypotension.
⚠️ Spironolactone + ACE inhibitor: Both raise K⁺ → hyperkalemia risk — watch potassium closely!
⚠️ Warfarin food trap: Consistent vitamin K, not zero vitamin K. Teach consistency, not avoidance.
⚠️ HIT paradox: Heparin causes thrombocytopenia that INCREASES clotting risk — it's counterintuitive!
⚠️ Daily weight = fluid status: 1 kg overnight = ~1 L fluid retained. Report >2 lb gain in 24 hrs to provider.

⭐ Professor Favorites

✔ Digoxin apical pulse check is ALWAYS required before administration

✔ ACE inhibitors → cough is expected — switch to ARB if intolerable

✔ Alteplase = thrombolytic (DISSOLVES clots); anticoagulants PREVENT clots — different mechanisms!

✔ Ejection fraction <40% = reduced EF = systolic heart failure = digoxin helps

✔ Furosemide → potassium depletes → increases digoxin toxicity risk

✔ Beta blockers: do NOT stop abruptly — rebound tachycardia/hypertension

✔ Left HF = wet lungs (SOB). Right HF = wet body (edema, JVD)

🔢 Critical Lab Numbers

INR therapeutic: 2–3
INR >3.5 = hold warfarin, assess bleeding
aPTT therapeutic on heparin: 60–100 sec
Normal aPTT: 25–35 sec
Platelets: 150,000–400,000/mm³
<50,000 = bleeding risk; <20,000 = critical
Troponin: should be undetectable
Any elevation = myocardial injury
K⁺ normal: 3.5–5.0 mEq/L
<3.5 = hypokalemia (furosemide risk) → digoxin toxicity ↑

📍 MONA for MI

M — Morphine (pain relief, reduce SNS demand)
O — Oxygen (if SpO₂ <94%)
N — Nitroglycerin (vasodilate coronaries, ↓ preload)
A — Aspirin 325 mg (antiplatelet — chew, don't swallow)

Always sit patient up and give aspirin ASAP
